Africa Safari Travel Guide

Planning an African safari raises hundreds of practical questions. This guide answers them in one place.

What should I pack? Do I need a visa? What happens when I land? How much cash should I carry? Will I have Wi-Fi? Can I use my phone? What should I wear? How much should I tip my guide? Are there bathrooms during game drives? What happens if it rains? Can children go on safari? What happens if I get sick? How much luggage can I take on a safari flight? Can I use a credit card? What happens during a typical safari day?

This Africa Safari Travel Guide is designed to answer those questions in one place.

It covers the practical side of traveling through Tanzania, Kenya, Botswana, Namibia, South Africa, Uganda, Rwanda, Zambia, Zimbabwe and Ethiopia, while pointing out where rules and conditions differ between countries.

The goal is simple: you should be able to use this guide to prepare for your safari without having to search the internet for basic practical information every time another question comes to mind.

Important: Immigration, health, airline, park and vaccination regulations can change. The information below explains how safari travel normally works, but travelers should verify time-sensitive entry and health requirements for their nationality and exact itinerary before departure.

1. Before You Book Your Safari

What should I decide before booking?

You do not need to know every detail before contacting a safari specialist, but these decisions make planning much easier:

  • Where you want to travel
  • Approximate travel dates
  • Number of travelers
  • Ages of children, if traveling as a family
  • Number of available days
  • Approximate budget
  • Wildlife you most want to see
  • Preferred accommodation level
  • Private or shared safari
  • Whether you want activities beyond game drives
  • Whether you want a beach extension
  • Whether you have mobility or dietary requirements

You do not need to know which lodge, park or route is best before asking for a quotation. That is part of the planning process.

How far in advance should I book?

There is no single booking deadline that works for every safari.

A simple safari during a quieter period may still be possible with relatively little notice. A honeymoon during peak season, a family trip during school holidays, a specific Great Migration period, or a safari requiring several small bush flights is much more dependent on availability.

Book earlier when you need:

  • A particular luxury lodge or camp
  • A specific family room or private villa
  • A private guide
  • A particular Migration period
  • Gorilla trekking permits
  • Kilimanjaro climbing
  • Balloon safaris
  • Internal flights
  • Connecting safari and beach arrangements
  • Multiple countries
  • Christmas or New Year travel
  • School-holiday travel

Booking early is particularly useful when the itinerary contains several limited-capacity services. Even if the park itself has space, one particular camp, flight or activity may not.

2. How Much Does an African Safari Cost?

There is no meaningful single price for an African safari.

Two seven-day safaris can have completely different prices because one may use simple lodges and road transport while the other uses luxury tented camps, private flights and private guides.

The most important factors are:

  • Accommodation
  • Number of travelers
  • Private or shared vehicle
  • Season
  • National park and conservation fees
  • Internal flights
  • Length of the safari
  • Special activities
  • Guide and vehicle arrangements
  • Beach or city extensions

Full per-day figures by tier sit in the safari cost guide.

Budget safari

Budget does not necessarily mean poor quality.

A well-planned budget safari can still provide excellent wildlife viewing. The main difference is usually the level of accommodation, location, room facilities, transport style and additional services.

Budget options may include:

  • Simple lodges
  • Campsites
  • Basic tented accommodation
  • Shared vehicles
  • Longer road transfers
  • Fewer premium activities

This style works particularly well for travelers who care more about spending time in the wilderness than having high-end rooms.

Mid-range safari

Mid-range is often the most practical choice for travelers who want comfortable accommodation without paying primarily for exclusivity.

Accommodation may include:

  • Permanent tented camps
  • Comfortable safari lodges
  • Private rooms with bathrooms
  • Good dining
  • Scenic locations
  • Swimming pools at selected properties
  • Reliable safari vehicles and guides

A mid-range safari can still feel very special because the quality of the experience depends heavily on the route and guide, not only the room.

Luxury safari

Luxury safaris place greater emphasis on location, privacy, service, design and the overall experience.

A luxury property may offer:

  • Large suites or luxury tents
  • Private decks
  • Outdoor showers
  • Bathtubs
  • Plunge pools
  • Exceptional views
  • Personalized service
  • Fine dining
  • Private or highly flexible game drives
  • Premium transfers
  • Remote locations

Luxury pricing is often influenced by the location of the camp as much as the room itself. A camp inside a prime wildlife area can save hours of driving compared with staying farther away.

Ultra-luxury safari

Ultra-luxury is aimed at travelers for whom exclusivity and personalization are major parts of the trip.

This can include:

  • Private-use villas
  • Exclusive-use safari houses
  • Personal staff
  • Private vehicles
  • Private guides
  • Charter flights
  • Highly remote camps
  • Private dining
  • Specialized experiences
  • Exceptional service-to-guest ratios

The difference between luxury and ultra-luxury is not simply the appearance of the room. It is usually the degree of privacy, personalization, exclusivity and logistical flexibility.

What does a safari quotation normally include?

A safari quotation may include some or all of the following:

  • Accommodation
  • Meals
  • Safari vehicle
  • Professional guide
  • Park entry fees
  • Conservation fees
  • Airport transfers
  • Internal flights
  • Bottled drinking water
  • Selected activities
  • Government taxes
  • Emergency evacuation coverage, where included by the operator

Always read the inclusions carefully.

A low headline price can become much higher if park fees, transfers, flights, activities and meals are excluded.

What may not be included?

Common exclusions include:

  • International flights
  • Travel insurance
  • Visa fees
  • Personal purchases
  • Alcoholic drinks
  • Laundry
  • Tips
  • Optional activities
  • Spa treatments
  • Premium beverages
  • Extra nights
  • Medical expenses
  • Personal communication costs

A good quotation should make these distinctions clear before you pay a deposit.

3. How Many Days Do I Need?

The right safari length depends more on the route than simply the number of parks.

Trying to visit five parks in four days may look attractive on paper but can create a trip dominated by driving and checking in and out.

2-3 days

Suitable for travelers with very limited time.

This works best when concentrating on one or two nearby wildlife areas.

The advantage is efficiency. The disadvantage is less flexibility when wildlife sightings take time.

4-5 days

A good introduction to safari.

This allows enough time to combine two or three complementary areas without making every day a transfer day.

6-8 days

Often the sweet spot for a first substantial safari.

You can spend multiple nights in major wildlife areas, allow time for different habitats and avoid changing accommodation every night.

9-14 days

Better for travelers who want variety.

A longer trip can combine:

  • Several ecosystems
  • Different countries
  • Wildlife and culture
  • Gorilla trekking
  • Kilimanjaro
  • A beach stay
  • Victoria Falls
  • Photography-focused experiences

The important principle is not to confuse more destinations with a better safari.

Three excellent nights in a productive wildlife area can be more rewarding than one night in three different places.

4. When Is the Best Time to Go on Safari?

There is no universal "best month" for Africa.

Different seasons favor different experiences.

Dry season

Dry periods are often popular because vegetation becomes thinner and animals may gather around remaining water sources.

This can make wildlife easier to locate in some ecosystems.

However, dry season also brings:

  • Higher demand
  • Higher accommodation prices in many areas
  • More vehicles around famous sightings
  • Hotter conditions in some regions

Green season

The rainy or green season is often misunderstood.

Rain does not necessarily mean that it rains all day.

Short storms can be followed by sunshine, landscapes become greener, migratory birds arrive, newborn animals may appear and photographic conditions can be spectacular.

Advantages can include:

  • Lower prices
  • Fewer travelers
  • Green landscapes
  • Excellent birding
  • Dramatic skies
  • Newborn wildlife
  • Strong photography opportunities

The trade-off is that some roads become more difficult and wildlife can be more dispersed.

Migration seasons

The Great Migration is not a single event occurring on one guaranteed date.

The herds move through the Serengeti-Mara ecosystem throughout the year. Calving, movement toward the west, northern movement and the return south happen at different times.

River crossings are particularly unpredictable. No responsible safari operator should promise a specific crossing on a particular day. Month-by-month detail sits in the best time to travel guide.

5. Weather: What Should I Expect?

African safari weather can vary considerably between regions.

Do not assume that the whole continent is hot throughout the year.

You may experience:

  • Hot afternoons
  • Cool early mornings
  • Cold nights
  • Heavy rain
  • Dry dusty conditions
  • Strong sunshine
  • High-altitude cold
  • Coastal humidity

Early morning game drives can feel surprisingly cold, particularly in open vehicles.

The same day may therefore require a jacket at sunrise and a hat and sunscreen by late morning.

This is why layered clothing is more useful than packing only heavy clothes or only shorts.

6. What Should I Pack for an African Safari?

You do not need an enormous suitcase.

The most useful safari wardrobe is practical, comfortable and easy to layer. The field-tested version of this list lives in the what to pack guide.

Clothing

Pack:

  • Lightweight trousers
  • Comfortable shorts
  • T-shirts
  • Long-sleeved shirts
  • Light fleece or sweater
  • Lightweight jacket
  • Underwear
  • Socks
  • Comfortable walking shoes
  • Sandals
  • Sleepwear
  • Swimwear
  • Hat or cap
  • Sunglasses

Neutral colors such as beige, brown, olive and grey are practical because they do not show dust as quickly and work well in the bush.

Bright clothing is not prohibited everywhere, but muted colors are generally more comfortable for wildlife activities.

Personal items

Bring:

  • Sunscreen
  • Insect repellent
  • Lip balm
  • Moisturizer
  • Personal medication
  • Basic first-aid supplies
  • Hand sanitizer
  • Reusable water bottle
  • Toiletries
  • Small flashlight or headlamp
  • Wet wipes
  • Power bank
  • Copies of important documents
  • Camera equipment

If photography matters to you, consider:

  • Camera body
  • Telephoto lens
  • Wide-angle lens
  • Extra batteries
  • Memory cards
  • Battery charger
  • Lens cleaning cloth
  • Dust protection
  • Beanbag or suitable camera support

A beanbag is particularly useful because safari vehicles provide different mounting opportunities from conventional photography trips.

7. How Much Luggage Can I Bring?

This is one of the most important questions when internal safari flights are involved.

Road safaris generally provide more flexibility than small aircraft.

Bush flights frequently use aircraft with limited luggage capacity, and some operators impose weight restrictions or require soft-sided bags.

Before packing, check:

  • Maximum total weight
  • Weight per bag
  • Whether hard suitcases are permitted
  • Whether camera equipment is treated separately
  • Hand-luggage limits
  • Whether excess luggage can be stored safely

Do not assume that the baggage allowance on your international flight is the same as the allowance on a small safari aircraft.

For itineraries involving several bush flights, soft-sided luggage is often the safest choice.

8. What Documents Should I Carry?

Keep the following accessible:

  • Passport
  • Visa or electronic travel authorization
  • International vaccination certificate where required
  • Travel insurance documents
  • Flight confirmations
  • Safari itinerary
  • Accommodation confirmations
  • Emergency contacts
  • Copies of prescriptions
  • Copies of important identification documents
  • Payment cards

Keep digital copies in secure cloud storage or email as well as offline copies on your phone.

It is also sensible to keep photocopies separate from your original documents.

9. Passports and Visas

Passport rules depend on nationality and destination.

A common requirement is a passport with at least six months of remaining validity, but travelers should always check the exact destination requirements before departure. The full country-by-country detail sits in the visa and vaccination guide.

For example, Tanzania's immigration authority currently states that its ordinary tourist visa is generally a single-entry visa valid for up to 90 days and lists a US$50 fee, while U.S. passport holders visiting for tourism use the multiple-entry category.

Kenya currently requires visitors, including infants and children, to obtain an approved Electronic Travel Authorisation before travel unless they fall under an exemption. Kenya also requires a passport valid for at least six months after planned arrival with at least one blank page.

Rwanda offers different visa arrangements depending on nationality and publishes visitor-visa options for tourism, including single- and multiple-entry arrangements.

Botswana's requirements depend on nationality. Where a visa is required, the government provides an online application route and advises travelers to have a passport valid for at least six months.

Zambia also separates travelers into visa-exempt, visa-on-arrival/mission and advance-visa categories. Its immigration authority states that passports should have at least six months' validity and three blank pages.

Do not use another traveler's visa requirements as your own. Two people traveling on different passports can have completely different entry rules.

10. Yellow Fever and Vaccinations

Yellow fever is particularly important because there are two separate questions:

  • Do I need the vaccine medically for the destination?
  • Does the destination require proof of vaccination because of where I am arriving from or transiting?

These are not always the same thing.

WHO notes that countries can require proof of yellow-fever vaccination from travelers arriving from risk areas, and requirements can change. The international vaccination certificate is recognized for lifelong validity after a valid vaccination.

Some travelers may have medical reasons not to receive the vaccine. Those cases should be discussed with a qualified travel-health professional.

Do not wait until the airport to discover that a vaccination certificate is required.

11. Malaria and Mosquito Protection

Malaria is present in many safari destinations, but the level of risk varies by country, region and altitude.

Protection normally involves several layers:

  • Avoiding mosquito bites
  • Using repellent
  • Wearing appropriate clothing
  • Sleeping under a mosquito net where provided
  • Using accommodation screening
  • Discussing preventive medication with a travel-health professional

CDC guidance recommends travelers to malaria-risk destinations discuss preventive medication and continue mosquito-bite prevention even when taking medication. Fever during or after travel to a malaria area requires prompt medical attention.

For Tanzania, for example, CDC guidance identifies malaria risk in areas below 1,800 metres, including Zanzibar, with preventive medication recommendations depending on the traveler and itinerary.

The correct medication is a medical decision. Do not take antimalarial drugs simply because another traveler used them.

12. Should I Get Travel Insurance?

Yes.

Travel insurance should be treated as part of safari preparation rather than an optional luxury.

A useful policy should be checked for:

  • Emergency medical treatment
  • Medical evacuation
  • Trip cancellation
  • Trip interruption
  • Lost baggage
  • Delayed baggage
  • Missed connections
  • Emergency repatriation
  • Adventure activities included in your itinerary

This becomes especially important when your trip involves:

  • Remote camps
  • Gorilla trekking
  • Kilimanjaro
  • Small aircraft
  • Multiple countries
  • Expensive non-refundable accommodation

Read the policy rather than assuming "travel insurance" automatically covers every activity.

13. What Happens If I Get Sick During Safari?

Do not assume you will be abandoned because you are far from a city.

Safari operators normally have procedures for dealing with illness and emergencies, although the exact response depends on location.

If you become unwell:

  • Tell your guide or camp staff immediately.
  • Explain your symptoms clearly.
  • Contact the appropriate medical provider.
  • Follow professional medical advice.
  • Arrange evacuation or transfer if medically necessary.

Do not hide symptoms because you are worried about interrupting the safari.

Fever, severe dehydration, breathing difficulties, serious injury, persistent vomiting or other significant symptoms require medical attention rather than simply waiting for them to disappear.

14. Emergency Evacuation

Emergency evacuation is different from ordinary travel insurance.

Some safari operators include emergency evacuation coverage or arrangements as part of the package. Other trips require travelers to purchase separate coverage.

Ask exactly what the policy covers.

Questions worth asking include:

  • Is air evacuation included?
  • Is ground evacuation included?
  • Where does evacuation take the traveler?
  • Is treatment included or only transportation?
  • Is the coverage valid throughout the itinerary?
  • Does it cover mountain trekking?
  • Does it cover gorilla trekking?
  • Are pre-existing medical conditions excluded?

This distinction can matter enormously when traveling in remote areas.

15. What Is a Typical Safari Day Like?

A safari day is usually built around wildlife activity rather than a fixed urban-style schedule.

A typical full-day structure may look like:

  • 05:30-06:30: Wake-up and preparation.
  • 06:00-07:00: Early morning departure.
  • Morning: Game viewing while temperatures are cooler and wildlife is often active.
  • Late morning: Breakfast or picnic depending on the itinerary.
  • Midday: Return to camp, lunch and rest, or continue with a full-day game drive.
  • Afternoon: Another game-viewing period.
  • Late afternoon: Wildlife viewing during softer light.
  • Evening: Return to camp before the relevant park or conservancy deadline.

This is not a rigid schedule.

Some days are designed around long game drives. Others allow time at the lodge or include walking, boating, cultural activities or other experiences.

16. Why Are Safari Drives Often Early in the Morning?

Wildlife does not follow the traveler's timetable.

Many animals are more active during cooler periods.

Early morning also offers:

  • Cooler temperatures
  • Softer light
  • Fewer people on the road
  • Predator activity
  • Better photographic conditions
  • Opportunity to see animals before they retreat into shade

This is why safari holidays can feel different from conventional vacations.

You may have to wake early, but the reward can be one of the most productive periods of the day.

17. Do I Have to Go on Every Game Drive?

No.

A safari is not a test of endurance.

If you have been on several long drives and want to sleep in, enjoy breakfast slowly or use the swimming pool, that can be part of the experience.

However, if wildlife viewing is your priority, skipping every morning drive means missing some of the most productive hours.

The best itinerary balances:

  • Wildlife viewing
  • Rest
  • Meals
  • Activities
  • Travel time
  • Personal preferences

This is one reason private safaris can be particularly useful for families, photographers and honeymooners.

18. What Is a Private Safari?

A private safari generally means that your vehicle and guide are reserved for your traveling party rather than shared with unrelated guests.

It gives you greater control over:

  • Departure time
  • Time spent at sightings
  • Meal stops
  • Photography
  • Pace
  • Breaks
  • Route decisions within permitted areas

It does not mean that you own the national park or can ignore park regulations.

You must still follow:

  • Speed restrictions
  • Road restrictions
  • Wildlife-distance rules
  • Park opening and closing times
  • Off-road regulations
  • Rules governing where guests may leave vehicles

19. What Is a Shared or Group Safari?

A shared safari combines travelers who may not know one another in the same vehicle.

The main advantage is price.

The trade-off is reduced flexibility.

For example, one traveler may want to stay with a leopard for another 30 minutes while another passenger wants to return to camp.

Shared safaris can be excellent for:

  • Solo travelers
  • Budget-conscious travelers
  • Social travelers
  • Shorter trips
  • Travelers who do not require complete flexibility

A private safari is generally better when the itinerary needs to revolve around a specific family's, couple's or photographer's preferences. Group departures are listed under group safaris.

20. What Is the Safari Vehicle Like?

Safari vehicles vary considerably.

Common types include:

  • 4x4 safari vehicles
  • Extended-wheelbase vehicles
  • Pop-up-roof vehicles
  • Open-sided vehicles
  • Minivans in selected destinations
  • Specialized photographic vehicles

A good safari vehicle should provide:

  • Good visibility
  • Comfortable seating
  • Space for personal equipment
  • Reliable communication
  • Drinking water
  • Appropriate suspension for rough roads

If photography is important, ask about window arrangements, seat configuration, camera support and vehicle capacity before booking.

21. Can I Get Out of the Safari Vehicle?

Usually only in designated or authorized places.

You should never assume that a quiet-looking landscape is safe simply because you cannot see animals nearby.

Wild animals can be hidden in vegetation, behind vehicles or close to water.

Your guide will tell you where it is safe to leave the vehicle.

Walking safaris have different procedures and are conducted under the supervision of trained guides or rangers where permitted.

22. Are Safari Parks Safe?

Wildlife areas are safe when visitors respect the rules and understand that they are entering a natural environment rather than a zoo.

The most important rule is simple:

Wild animals are wild.

Never:

  • Feed animals
  • Call animals toward the vehicle
  • Shout at wildlife
  • Stand unnecessarily in the vehicle
  • Approach animals on foot
  • Leave the vehicle without permission
  • Chase animals
  • Block animals from moving
  • Use flash irresponsibly
  • Throw objects
  • Litter

Park regulations differ by destination. For example, Tanzania's national park authority publishes official visitor guidelines and procedures for safe park visits.

23. How Close Will We Get to Wildlife?

There is no guaranteed distance.

Your guide will position the vehicle according to the animal, terrain, park rules and behavior of the wildlife.

The best guides understand that a good sighting is not always the closest sighting.

Sometimes an animal moving naturally away from a vehicle is more valuable than one that has been pressured into changing its behavior.

Responsible viewing protects both the wildlife and the quality of the experience.

24. Can I Take Photographs and Videos?

Yes, photography is one of the major attractions of safari.

However, wildlife photography requires patience.

Good photographs depend on:

  • Light
  • Position
  • Animal behavior
  • Background
  • Vehicle position
  • Lens choice
  • Patience
  • Timing

Do not expect every sighting to produce a perfect photograph.

Avoid loud camera shutters, flash where inappropriate and constant movement around the vehicle.

If photography is one of your main reasons for traveling, tell your safari planner before booking. A photographic itinerary can be designed differently from an ordinary sightseeing safari - see the photographic safaris options.

25. How Does Wi-Fi and Mobile Internet Work?

Connectivity varies dramatically.

Luxury lodges often have Wi-Fi, while remote camps may have limited connectivity or none at all.

You may experience:

  • Strong Wi-Fi in cities
  • Good connectivity around some lodges
  • Intermittent mobile service
  • No signal in remote wilderness areas

Do not build your entire trip around continuous internet access.

Download important documents, maps, entertainment and contact information before leaving major cities.

An eSIM or local SIM can be useful where networks are available.

26. Can I Charge My Phone and Camera?

Most established lodges and camps provide charging facilities.

However, remote camps may have:

  • Limited electricity hours
  • Solar power
  • Central charging stations
  • USB charging
  • Generator-powered electricity

Carry a power bank for your phone and consider carrying spare camera batteries.

If you are a serious photographer, charging strategy becomes especially important during multi-day remote safaris.

27. Can I Use My Credit Card?

Credit cards are widely useful in cities, airports, larger hotels and many established lodges.

They should not, however, be your only payment method.

Carry some local currency and/or widely accepted foreign cash for:

  • Tips
  • Small purchases
  • Markets
  • Remote areas
  • Small restaurants
  • Emergency situations

Always inform your bank that you are traveling internationally if your bank requires travel notifications.

28. How Much Cash Should I Carry?

Avoid carrying your entire holiday budget in cash.

Instead, carry enough for:

  • Tips
  • Small purchases
  • Emergency expenses
  • Places where cards may not work

The amount depends on your travel style.

If most accommodation, meals, transport and activities are prepaid, your daily cash requirement may be relatively small.

29. Should I Carry US Dollars?

US dollars are widely useful across the safari industry, particularly for tourism-related payments and tips, but local-currency requirements and accepted notes vary.

Bring clean, undamaged notes and check the payment preferences of your operator.

Do not assume that every small business accepts foreign currency.

It is also important to understand whether a quoted price is:

  • Per person
  • Per night
  • Per vehicle
  • Per room
  • Per activity
  • Total for the group

This avoids misunderstandings when comparing quotations.

30. How Does Tipping Work?

Tipping practices vary.

A tip should reward good service rather than be treated as a compulsory payment for every interaction.

You may encounter:

  • Guide tips
  • Driver-guide tips
  • Camp staff tips
  • Restaurant tips
  • Porter tips
  • Special activity tips

Many safari lodges use a communal or shared staff tip box so that kitchen, housekeeping and support staff also benefit.

For a private safari, many travelers tip the guide directly because the guide has a major influence on the experience.

Ask your safari operator for destination-specific tipping guidance before departure - the tipping guide has the usual ranges.

31. What Food Should I Expect?

Safari accommodation commonly provides meals based on international and local cuisine.

Typical meals may include:

  • Fresh fruit
  • Eggs
  • Bread
  • Cereals
  • Vegetables
  • Rice
  • Potatoes
  • Pasta
  • Meat
  • Fish
  • Soups
  • Salads
  • Desserts

Remote camps can have surprisingly sophisticated menus.

If you are vegetarian, vegan, gluten-free, halal, kosher or have food allergies, tell the operator before travel.

Do not wait until dinner to mention a serious allergy.

32. Can I Drink the Water?

Do not assume that tap water is safe everywhere.

Safari lodges commonly provide treated or bottled drinking water.

Follow the accommodation's guidance regarding:

  • Drinking water
  • Ice
  • Brushing teeth
  • Food hygiene

If you have a sensitive stomach, it is sensible to follow the most conservative advice provided by your accommodation and travel-health professional.

33. What About Bathrooms During Game Drives?

This is one of the practical questions many first-time travelers hesitate to ask.

During a game drive, bathroom access depends on the route.

You may have access to:

  • Lodge bathrooms
  • Camp bathrooms
  • Designated park facilities
  • Bush toilet arrangements where permitted and handled safely

Do not simply leave the vehicle and walk into the bush because you need a bathroom.

Tell your guide.

They know where stopping is appropriate and safe.

34. What Happens If It Rains?

Safari does not automatically stop because of rain.

In many areas, rain can make the landscape spectacular.

However, heavy rainfall can affect:

  • Road conditions
  • River crossings
  • Driving times
  • Airstrips
  • Accessibility of camps
  • Game visibility

Safari vehicles normally carry equipment to deal with changing weather, but travelers should still keep a rain jacket easily accessible.

Do not bury your rain jacket at the bottom of your luggage.

35. Can I Go on Safari With Children?

Yes, but the itinerary should be designed around the children's ages.

Young children may struggle with:

  • Long drives
  • Early departures
  • Heat
  • Waiting quietly
  • Several hours in a vehicle

Older children and teenagers often enjoy safari enormously, especially when guides explain tracks, insects, animal behavior, conservation and local culture.

Age restrictions are not universal.

Some parks, walking activities, camps and guided experiences have minimum ages. For example, TANAPA rules place specific age restrictions on some higher-altitude and walking activities, while SANParks also applies minimum ages to certain guided activities in Kruger.

Always check the exact activity rather than assuming that "children are allowed on safari" means they can participate in every experience. The family safari guide covers ages, rooms and pacing in detail.

36. Is Safari Suitable for Older Travelers?

Yes.

Safari can work very well for older travelers when the itinerary is designed around comfort.

Consider:

  • Shorter transfers
  • Fewer accommodation changes
  • Comfortable vehicles
  • Ground-floor rooms where appropriate
  • Easy-access accommodation
  • Private vehicles
  • Domestic flights instead of long road journeys
  • Time for rest
  • Medical considerations
  • Travel insurance

The most demanding part of a safari is often not the wildlife viewing but the road conditions and long transfers.

Good planning can reduce unnecessary physical strain.

37. Is Safari Suitable for Travelers With Mobility Limitations?

It can be, but it requires advance planning.

Tell the safari planner about:

  • Wheelchair use
  • Walking difficulties
  • Stairs
  • Balance problems
  • Difficulty entering vehicles
  • Need for accessible bathrooms
  • Medication requirements

Not every safari lodge is designed for wheelchair accessibility.

Likewise, some small aircraft have narrow boarding areas and luggage limitations.

The earlier these requirements are discussed, the more realistic the itinerary can be.

38. What Should Honeymooners Know?

A honeymoon safari should not simply be a normal safari with the word "honeymoon" added to it.

The pace should usually allow privacy and relaxation.

Consider combining:

  • Private game drives
  • Luxury accommodation
  • Bush dinners
  • Sundowners
  • Private dining
  • Plunge pools
  • Spa treatments
  • Scenic rooms
  • A beach extension

A safari can be physically active, so adding several relaxing nights at the end can make the overall trip feel much more balanced.

A Tanzania safari followed by Zanzibar, for example, can combine wildlife with a slower coastal stay. Honeymoon-specific planning sits on the honeymoon safaris page.

39. Should I Combine Safari With a Beach Holiday?

Yes, if you have enough time.

Common combinations include:

  • Safari + Zanzibar
  • Safari + Kenya coast
  • Safari + Mozambique
  • Safari + South African coast
  • Safari + Lake Malawi
  • Safari + Victoria Falls

The best combination depends on geography.

Avoid adding a beach destination simply because it sounds attractive if doing so creates unnecessary flights and long transfers. The safari and Zanzibar combinations are the most straightforward from East Africa.

40. Can I Visit More Than One African Country?

Absolutely.

Multi-country safaris can be excellent when the destinations complement one another.

Examples include:

  • Kenya + Tanzania
  • Botswana + Zimbabwe
  • Botswana + Namibia
  • Zambia + Zimbabwe
  • Rwanda + Tanzania
  • Uganda + Kenya
  • South Africa + Botswana
  • Tanzania + Zanzibar

The challenge is logistics.

Every border crossing or flight introduces another point where delays can occur.

A two-country safari should therefore be planned around the actual transport network rather than simply connecting destinations on a map.

41. How Do Internal Safari Flights Work?

Small aircraft are an important part of many African safaris.

They allow travelers to replace long road transfers with relatively short flights to remote airstrips.

The aircraft may be:

  • Small bush planes
  • Turboprops
  • Scheduled safari aircraft
  • Charter aircraft

You may land on a gravel or dirt airstrip rather than a conventional airport.

A safari vehicle from your camp normally meets the flight.

Passengers should follow luggage restrictions carefully.

42. Are Safari Flights Safe?

Aviation safety should always be taken seriously.

Use established operators that meet the applicable aviation requirements of the country in which they operate.

Before booking, travelers can ask:

  • Which airline operates the flight?
  • Is it scheduled or charter?
  • What aircraft type is used?
  • What is the baggage allowance?
  • Is the flight direct?
  • Are there intermediate stops?
  • What happens if weather delays the flight?

Weather can affect bush flights, particularly during severe storms.

43. What Happens When My International Flight Is Delayed?

This is one reason itinerary design matters.

If you arrive late and have an immediate domestic flight, a delay can affect the entire safari.

Whenever possible, allow enough connection time between:

  • International arrival
  • Immigration
  • Baggage collection
  • Domestic terminal transfer
  • Check-in
  • Security
  • Boarding

For important safari connections, do not build an itinerary that depends on a few minutes of spare time.

44. What Happens If My Luggage Is Delayed?

Report delayed baggage immediately to the airline.

Keep your baggage tag.

Your safari operator should be informed because your itinerary may move to a remote location before your bag arrives.

This is why essential items should go into your hand luggage:

  • Medication
  • Passport
  • Travel documents
  • Phone
  • Chargers
  • One change of clothing
  • Basic toiletries
  • Important camera equipment

Never put essential medication into checked baggage.

45. What Should I Keep in My Hand Luggage?

A useful hand-luggage checklist:

  • Passport
  • Visa documents
  • Wallet
  • Phone
  • Charging cable
  • Power bank
  • Medication
  • Prescription
  • Travel insurance information
  • Camera
  • Camera batteries
  • Memory cards
  • One change of clothes
  • Basic toiletries
  • Glasses/contact lenses
  • Important documents

Keep valuables with you rather than in checked luggage.

46. What Language Will My Guide Speak?

English is widely used in the safari industry across many destinations.

French, German, Spanish and other languages can also be arranged depending on the destination and operator.

If you require a specific language, mention it before booking.

A language-specific guide may need to be reserved in advance, particularly during busy periods.

47. What Makes a Good Safari Guide?

A good guide is not simply someone who can identify animals.

Strong guiding involves:

  • Wildlife knowledge
  • Tracking skills
  • Understanding animal behavior
  • Knowledge of the ecosystem
  • Safe driving
  • Communication
  • Patience
  • Photography awareness
  • Local cultural knowledge
  • Flexibility
  • Respect for wildlife

The guide should help you understand why something is happening, not simply tell you the animal's name.

A lion sighting becomes more interesting when you understand why the pride is positioned where it is, what it has been hunting and how its behavior changes throughout the day.

48. Should I Choose a Private Guide?

A private guide is particularly useful if:

  • You want flexibility
  • You are a photographer
  • You are traveling with children
  • You have mobility considerations
  • You want a particular language
  • You are celebrating a special occasion
  • You have specific wildlife interests

It is less important for travelers who are comfortable following a fixed schedule and sharing the experience with others.

49. What Safari Activities Can I Do Besides Game Drives?

Depending on the destination, activities may include:

  • Walking safaris
  • Boat safaris
  • Canoeing
  • Hot-air balloon flights
  • Cultural visits
  • Village visits
  • Birdwatching
  • Photography
  • Fishing
  • Mountain trekking
  • Gorilla trekking
  • Chimpanzee trekking
  • Horseback riding
  • Scenic flights
  • Conservation experiences

Not every activity is available everywhere.

Some have minimum age requirements or require specialist guides.

50. Can I Go on a Walking Safari?

Yes, where permitted.

Walking offers a completely different perspective.

Instead of concentrating only on large animals, you begin noticing:

  • Tracks
  • Droppings
  • Insects
  • Plants
  • Birds
  • Animal calls
  • Smaller mammals
  • Changes in the landscape

Walking safaris are normally conducted under specific safety procedures.

Never assume that because you have walked near animals elsewhere you can independently walk through a wildlife area.

51. What Should I Know About Gorilla Trekking?

Gorilla trekking is substantially different from an ordinary game drive.

You may hike through steep, muddy or dense forest before finding the gorillas.

The physical difficulty can vary significantly from one trek to another.

Standard gorilla trekking in Rwanda and Uganda generally has a minimum age of 15, and viewing time is limited so that the animals are not subjected to prolonged human presence.

Gorilla trekking also requires careful disease precautions because respiratory infections can be dangerous to gorillas.

Travelers should never participate while ill.

52. What Should I Know About Climbing Kilimanjaro?

Kilimanjaro is a high-altitude mountain, not simply a long walk.

The summit reaches 5,895 metres.

Altitude is therefore one of the most important considerations.

A good climb should allow sufficient time for acclimatization rather than choosing the shortest itinerary simply because it is cheaper.

Important preparation includes:

  • Physical training
  • Proper clothing
  • Good footwear
  • Hydration
  • Layering
  • Understanding altitude symptoms
  • Choosing a suitable route
  • Using a licensed operator
  • Following your mountain guide's instructions

If you develop serious altitude symptoms, inform your guide immediately.

53. What Should I Know About Cultural Visits?

Cultural experiences can add considerable depth to a safari when they are handled respectfully.

A genuine cultural visit should not treat communities as a performance.

Travelers should:

  • Ask before taking photographs
  • Respect religious and cultural practices
  • Dress appropriately
  • Pay for goods or experiences where expected
  • Avoid intrusive questions
  • Listen to local guides
  • Avoid giving money or gifts directly to children unless the activity is designed around responsible distribution

The best cultural experiences allow travelers to understand local life rather than simply take photographs.

54. Can I Buy Souvenirs?

Yes.

Common purchases include:

  • Handcrafted baskets
  • Beaded work
  • Textiles
  • Carvings
  • Artwork
  • Jewelry
  • Local crafts

When buying wildlife-related products, make sure they are legal and ethically sourced.

Never purchase products made from protected wildlife or anything whose legal origin is uncertain.

55. How Should I Behave Around Local Communities?

Treat people with the same respect you would expect visitors to show toward your own community.

Ask before photographing people.

Do not enter homes or private spaces without permission.

Do not assume that poverty gives you permission to photograph someone.

When visiting a village or community project, listen to your local guide regarding appropriate behavior.

56. What Should I Know About Responsible Safari Travel?

Responsible tourism is not only about avoiding litter.

It also includes:

  • Respecting wildlife
  • Staying on permitted roads
  • Avoiding unnecessary disturbance
  • Supporting local employment
  • Using community-based tourism where appropriate
  • Reducing plastic waste
  • Respecting local communities
  • Choosing ethical wildlife experiences
  • Supporting conservation
  • Following park regulations

A responsible safari protects the experience for future travelers as well as the wildlife itself.

57. Is Plastic a Problem?

Many safari destinations have restrictions concerning plastic bags and waste.

Avoid unnecessary single-use plastic.

Carry reusable items where practical and follow local regulations.

Never leave:

  • Bottles
  • Wrappers
  • Tissues
  • Food packaging
  • Batteries
  • Cigarette butts

in the wilderness.

A small piece of litter that looks insignificant to a traveler can remain in an ecosystem for a very long time.

58. What Should I Know About Smoking?

Smoking is generally restricted in vehicles, accommodation areas and wildlife viewing situations.

Never throw cigarette ends onto the ground.

Fire is a serious risk in dry wilderness areas.

Follow your guide's instructions regarding where smoking is permitted.

59. Is Alcohol Available?

Many lodges and camps serve alcoholic drinks.

Remote or budget properties may have a more limited selection.

Alcohol should never interfere with wildlife safety.

Never become intoxicated before a game drive, walking safari or other activity where alertness is important.

60. Can I Travel Solo?

Yes.

Solo travel can work particularly well when joining a small group or booking a private safari.

Solo travelers should pay attention to:

  • Single supplements
  • Shared versus private vehicles
  • Internal flight costs
  • Accommodation room rates
  • Security arrangements
  • Transfer logistics

A private safari can be expensive for one traveler because the vehicle and guide costs are not divided among several people. See the solo safari guide for how the numbers usually work.

61. Is Africa Safari Suitable for Couples?

Very much so.

Couples can choose between:

  • Shared safaris
  • Private safaris
  • Luxury camps
  • Photography trips
  • Adventure-focused itineraries
  • Honeymoon itineraries
  • Safari and beach combinations

The important question is not simply "which country?" but what kind of experience the couple wants.

62. How Do I Choose the Right Destination?

Start with the experience rather than the country.

  • If your priority is the Great Migration: consider the Serengeti and Maasai Mara ecosystem.
  • If you want elephants: consider destinations known for large elephant populations, including Tarangire, Amboseli, Chobe, Hwange and parts of Botswana and Zimbabwe.
  • If you want gorillas: look toward Rwanda and Uganda.
  • If you want chimpanzees: Uganda and Rwanda provide strong options.
  • If you want walking safaris: consider Zambia and selected areas of Botswana, Zimbabwe and Tanzania.
  • If you want water-based safari: consider the Okavango Delta, Chobe, Lower Zambezi and selected Tanzanian waterways.
  • If you want a safari and beach: Tanzania and Zanzibar are one of the most straightforward combinations, while Kenya also offers strong safari-and-coast itineraries.
  • If you want a self-drive safari: Namibia and South Africa are particularly well suited to travelers who enjoy independent road travel.

63. How Do I Choose the Right Park?

Do not choose a park only because you have seen its name repeatedly online.

Consider:

  • Wildlife species
  • Landscape
  • Season
  • Accessibility
  • Tourism levels
  • Accommodation
  • Activities
  • Budget
  • Number of nights
  • Your tolerance for long drives

For example, one traveler may prefer a park famous for large open plains while another may prefer woodland, wetlands, mountains or river environments.

The "best" park depends on what you want to experience - the national parks hub reviews 32 of them honestly, park by park.

64. How Many Parks Should I Visit?

Fewer parks with more time can often produce a better safari.

Moving accommodation every night creates:

  • Packing
  • Check-outs
  • Road transfers
  • Check-ins
  • Less time watching wildlife

A strong itinerary usually has a reason for each destination.

For example:

  • Park A: elephants and dry-season wildlife
  • Park B: migration and predators
  • Park C: crater or specialized ecosystem

That creates diversity without unnecessary movement.

65. Should I Choose a Lodge or Tented Camp?

Both can be excellent.

Safari lodge

Usually a permanent structure with:

  • Solid walls
  • Conventional rooms
  • Restaurant
  • Lounge
  • Pool at some properties

Tented camp

Safari tents can range from simple accommodation to extremely luxurious suites.

A high-end tent can have:

  • Proper beds
  • Private bathroom
  • Hot shower
  • Flush toilet
  • Wooden floors
  • Large deck
  • Outdoor shower
  • Bathtub
  • Plunge pool

"Tent" does not automatically mean basic. Types, tiers and trade-offs are set out on the accommodation page.

66. What Does "Mobile Safari Camp" Mean?

A mobile camp moves according to the safari itinerary.

The concept can place travelers closer to particular wildlife areas and provide a more expedition-style experience.

The level of comfort varies considerably.

Some mobile camps are simple and adventurous; others provide high-quality beds, private bathrooms and excellent meals.

Ask exactly what facilities are included before booking.

67. What Is an Exclusive-Use Villa?

An exclusive-use villa or safari house is booked by one group.

It can be particularly useful for:

  • Families
  • Multigenerational groups
  • Friends
  • Celebrations
  • Honeymoons
  • Travelers wanting maximum privacy

The accommodation may include private staff, dining arrangements and a dedicated vehicle.

This is different from simply booking a private safari vehicle while staying in a shared lodge.

68. What Does Full Board Mean?

Full board normally means:

  • Breakfast
  • Lunch
  • Dinner

The exact drinks and extras vary.

Some properties include selected non-alcoholic drinks and others may include a wider range.

Always check the property's specific meal and beverage policy.

69. Are Drinks Included?

Not automatically.

Depending on the property, the package may include:

  • Drinking water
  • Tea
  • Coffee
  • Soft drinks
  • Selected alcoholic beverages

Premium wines, spirits and special drinks may cost extra.

Ask before ordering if you are traveling on a controlled budget.

70. What Is the Difference Between Park Fees and Conservation Fees?

They are related but not necessarily identical.

A destination may charge:

  • Park entry fees
  • Conservation fees
  • Community fees
  • Concession fees
  • Vehicle fees
  • Activity fees

The exact structure depends on the destination and authority.

This is one reason safari quotations can look expensive compared with ordinary hotel holidays: the cost of accessing and operating within protected wildlife areas forms a significant part of the trip.

71. Why Are Some Safaris More Expensive Than Others?

A safari's price is affected by more than accommodation.

Two camps at a similar room rate can produce very different total prices because one requires:

  • A domestic flight
  • A conservation fee
  • A concession fee
  • A private transfer
  • A specialist guide

while the other can be reached by road.

Location can therefore have a major impact on total cost.

72. What Is a Private Concession?

A private concession is a specially managed wildlife area, often associated with a lodge or group of lodges.

Depending on local regulations, private concessions can offer activities that are more restricted inside national parks, such as:

  • Night drives
  • Off-road viewing
  • Walking

The exact rules differ by destination.

Do not assume that an activity permitted in one concession is legal in another.

73. Are Night Game Drives Available?

Sometimes.

Night driving is destination-specific.

Some national parks prohibit ordinary night driving, while private concessions or designated operators may offer it under controlled conditions.

Night drives can reveal:

  • Nocturnal predators
  • Genets
  • Civets
  • Bushbabies
  • Owls
  • Nightjars
  • Hyenas
  • Other nocturnal species

If night wildlife is important to you, choose the destination specifically for that opportunity rather than expecting every safari to include it.

74. What If I Don't See the Big Five?

Wildlife is not a zoo.

There is no guarantee that you will see:

  • Lion
  • Leopard
  • Elephant
  • Buffalo
  • Rhino

A safari should be judged by the quality and diversity of the overall experience, not only by checking five names off a list.

A traveler who sees lions, elephants, giraffes, wild dogs, hyenas, birds, hippos and a spectacular sunset may have a better safari than someone who sees four Big Five species for five minutes each.

75. Why Can't My Guide Guarantee a Leopard?

Leopards are naturally difficult to find.

They are often:

  • Solitary
  • Well camouflaged
  • Active at different times
  • Comfortable in dense vegetation
  • Difficult to spot from a moving vehicle

A good guide can improve your chances through tracking, local knowledge and communication with other guides.

But no ethical guide should promise a wild animal on demand.

76. What Should I Do If Other Vehicles Crowd a Sighting?

Crowding can happen around famous wildlife sightings.

A private guide can sometimes reposition or wait, depending on the rules and animal behavior.

Do not pressure the guide to drive illegally or off-road merely to obtain a photograph.

Sometimes leaving a crowded sighting produces a better experience elsewhere.

77. What Happens During a Long Driving Day?

Long road days are sometimes unavoidable.

The vehicle normally stops for:

  • Fuel
  • Bathrooms
  • Meals
  • Stretching
  • Scenic views
  • Immigration or border formalities where applicable

Wear comfortable clothing and keep water accessible.

If you dislike long road transfers, tell the planner before the itinerary is designed. Domestic flights can sometimes transform the experience.

78. Should I Choose Road or Fly-In Safari?

Choose road travel when:

  • You enjoy landscapes
  • You want to see rural life
  • Your route is geographically compact
  • You want to control costs
  • You enjoy the journey itself

Choose flights when:

  • Distances are large
  • You have limited time
  • Camps are remote
  • You want to maximize wildlife time
  • Long road journeys would reduce your enjoyment

The ideal itinerary can combine both.

79. What Should I Know About Border Crossings?

Multi-country safaris require additional logistics.

You may need:

  • Passport
  • Visa
  • Proof of accommodation
  • Yellow fever certificate
  • Immigration forms
  • Vehicle documentation for road crossings
  • Appropriate entry permissions

Border procedures can take time.

Do not schedule an important international flight immediately after a land-border crossing unless the itinerary has enough contingency time.

80. Can I Use My Own Drone?

Do not assume that you can fly a drone over wildlife areas.

Drones may be restricted or prohibited by:

  • National park authorities
  • Aviation authorities
  • Conservation areas
  • Private properties
  • Military/security regulations

A drone can also disturb wildlife.

Always obtain the necessary permissions before bringing one into a protected area.

81. Can I Use a Drone for Safari Photography?

Even where aviation rules technically permit drones, wildlife rules may prohibit their use.

Never launch a drone simply because you see another traveler doing so.

A protected area can have rules that are stricter than the country's general drone regulations.

82. What Should I Know About Electricity?

Power systems differ.

Bring the appropriate plug adapter for your itinerary.

Some remote camps operate on:

  • Solar power
  • Generators
  • Battery systems

Electricity may be limited at night.

If you depend on medical equipment that requires continuous electricity, discuss this before booking.

83. Can I Wash Clothes During Safari?

Many lodges offer laundry services.

However:

  • Small camps may have limited laundry capacity
  • Some properties may not wash underwear
  • Drying depends on weather
  • Same-day service may not always be available
  • Laundry may cost extra

Packing light and using laundry services can be more practical than carrying a large wardrobe.

84. What Shoes Should I Bring?

For ordinary game drives, comfortable closed shoes are usually enough.

For walking safaris or trekking, footwear should be selected according to the terrain.

Good safari footwear should be:

  • Comfortable
  • Already broken in
  • Secure around the foot
  • Suitable for uneven surfaces

Do not bring brand-new hiking boots and expect them to be comfortable on the first long walk.

85. Do I Need Hiking Boots?

Not necessarily.

For a vehicle-based safari, lightweight walking shoes or trainers are often sufficient.

For:

  • Gorilla trekking
  • Kilimanjaro
  • Long walking safaris
  • Mountain trekking

more supportive footwear may be appropriate.

The activity determines the footwear, not the word "safari."

86. What About Sun Protection?

Sun exposure can be significant.

Bring:

  • High-SPF sunscreen
  • Sunglasses
  • Hat
  • Lip protection
  • Lightweight long sleeves

Drink water regularly, particularly during long drives.

Heat exhaustion can turn an enjoyable safari into a medical problem very quickly.

87. What About Insects?

Mosquitoes are not the only insects you may encounter.

You may also see:

  • Flies
  • Ants
  • Beetles
  • Moths
  • Bees
  • Other harmless insects

Most are simply part of the ecosystem.

Keep food sealed, follow accommodation instructions and use insect repellent where appropriate.

Do not kill insects unnecessarily inside wilderness areas.

88. What About Snakes?

Snakes are present in many African ecosystems, but travelers generally encounter them far less frequently than television might suggest.

The best approach is simple:

  • Do not approach snakes
  • Do not attempt to identify them yourself at close range
  • Follow your guide's instructions
  • Watch where you step during walking activities
  • Do not put your hands into holes or vegetation

Your guide is trained to assess wildlife situations.

89. What About Wild Animals Around the Lodge?

Animals may sometimes move through or near camps.

You could encounter:

  • Monkeys
  • Antelope
  • Warthogs
  • Elephants
  • Hippos
  • Baboons
  • Birds

This is one reason many camps require guests to be escorted after dark.

Do not walk alone at night simply because the distance to your room looks short.

90. What Should I Do Around Monkeys and Baboons?

Do not feed them.

Do not leave food unattended.

Do not leave room doors open if the property warns against it.

Do not try to touch them.

They are intelligent wild animals and can become aggressive when they associate people with food.

91. What Should I Know About Hippos?

Hippos may appear calm, but they are powerful wild animals.

Never approach them on foot.

At camps near rivers or lakes, follow staff instructions regarding movement after dark.

The same principle applies to elephants and other large wildlife: distance and professional guidance matter.

92. What Should I Do If an Elephant Approaches the Vehicle?

Stay calm.

Remain inside the vehicle unless your guide tells you otherwise.

Do not shout, make sudden movements or attempt to attract the elephant.

Your guide will assess the elephant's behavior and position the vehicle appropriately.

A close encounter can be extraordinary precisely because the animal is behaving naturally.

93. Can I Leave the Safari Vehicle to Take a Better Photograph?

No, unless your guide confirms that you are in a designated safe location.

A photograph is never worth putting yourself or others at risk.

Safari photography should work around wildlife rather than forcing wildlife to work around the photographer.

94. What Happens If I Lose My Passport?

Contact your country's embassy or consulate immediately.

You may need:

  • Police report
  • Identification documents
  • Passport copy
  • Passport photographs
  • Proof of onward travel
  • Emergency travel document

This is why carrying a secure digital copy of your passport is useful.

Keep your physical passport secure and avoid handing it to unofficial individuals.

95. What Happens If My Phone Is Lost?

Use another device to:

  • Contact your bank
  • Block cards if necessary
  • Contact your insurance provider
  • Contact your operator
  • Access important documents
  • Contact family

Before traveling, make sure your important accounts can be recovered without relying entirely on the lost phone.

96. What Happens If I Miss a Flight?

Contact the airline and safari operator immediately.

Do not simply assume that the next flight will be available.

A missed flight can affect:

  • Domestic connections
  • Lodge transfers
  • Park entry
  • Activities
  • Accommodation nights

This is another reason to keep your safari operator's emergency contact information offline.

97. What Emergency Numbers Should I Save?

Save:

  • Safari operator emergency contact
  • Accommodation contact
  • Airline
  • Travel insurance emergency line
  • Your country's embassy/consulate
  • Local emergency services where appropriate
  • Family emergency contact

Do this before entering remote areas.

98. What Should I Know About Travel Insurance Claims?

Keep evidence.

Depending on the claim, you may need:

  • Receipts
  • Police reports
  • Airline delay documentation
  • Medical reports
  • Baggage reports
  • Booking confirmations
  • Proof of payment

Do not throw away baggage tags after a flight until you are certain your luggage has arrived safely.

99. What Should I Know About Photography Etiquette?

When photographing wildlife:

  • Avoid flash when inappropriate
  • Do not block other vehicles
  • Do not demand that the guide chase animals
  • Avoid shouting
  • Keep equipment secure
  • Give other travelers viewing opportunities
  • Respect restricted areas
  • Never use photography as an excuse to break wildlife rules

When photographing people:

Ask permission.

A photograph can be technically excellent and still be ethically inappropriate.

100. What Should I Know Before My First Game Drive?

Keep these items accessible:

  • Camera
  • Phone
  • Sunglasses
  • Hat
  • Jacket
  • Water
  • Sunscreen
  • Binoculars
  • Insect repellent
  • Personal medication

Do not put everything into the luggage compartment.

You may not return to the lodge for several hours.

101. Should I Bring Binoculars?

Yes.

Binoculars can make a major difference.

They are useful for:

  • Birds
  • Distant predators
  • Tree-dwelling animals
  • Landscapes
  • Animals across rivers
  • Wildlife that cannot safely be approached closely

A pair of compact binoculars can be more useful than travelers expect.

102. What If I Am Not Interested in Birds?

You may become interested once you are on safari.

Africa's bird diversity is enormous, and birds often provide activity between major mammal sightings.

Learning a few common species can also help you understand the ecosystem.

You do not need professional birding equipment unless birdwatching is a primary objective.

103. Can I Customize My Safari?

Yes.

A tailor-made safari can be adjusted around:

  • Dates
  • Budget
  • Wildlife priorities
  • Accommodation
  • Private/shared operation
  • Photography
  • Family needs
  • Honeymoon requirements
  • Activities
  • Beach stays
  • Flight preferences
  • Pace

Tailor-made does not necessarily mean luxury.

A customized safari can be budget, mid-range, luxury or ultra-luxury - see the tailor-made safaris page.

104. What Is the Difference Between Tailor-Made and Private?

These terms describe different things.

Tailor-made describes how the itinerary is designed.

Private describes how the safari is operated.

You can therefore have:

  • A tailor-made private safari
  • A tailor-made shared safari
  • A fixed private safari
  • A group safari with a customized extension

Understanding this distinction helps travelers compare quotations correctly.

105. How Can I Avoid Overpacking?

Start with your itinerary rather than your suitcase.

If the trip includes:

  • Safari
  • City
  • Beach
  • Mountain
  • Gorilla trekking

pack for the activities rather than bringing multiple outfits for every day.

Safari clothing is generally casual.

You do not need a completely new outfit every evening.

106. Do I Need Formal Clothes?

Usually not for safari.

Some luxury city hotels and high-end restaurants may have dress expectations, but most safari lodges are relaxed.

Smart-casual clothing is generally enough for evenings.

Comfort is usually more important than fashion in the bush.

107. What Should I Wear at Night?

Temperatures can fall significantly after sunset.

A useful evening combination is:

  • Long trousers
  • Long-sleeved shirt
  • Light fleece
  • Jacket if needed
  • Closed shoes

At coastal destinations, conditions are usually warmer and more humid.

At high elevations, especially around mountains, temperatures can be much colder.

108. Can I Bring Children on Walking Safaris?

Age restrictions depend on the destination and activity.

Walking in a wildlife area is fundamentally different from walking through a normal nature reserve.

Some authorities impose minimum ages because children must be able to follow instructions immediately and remain quiet when necessary.

Never assume that an activity offered to adults is automatically available to children.

109. What Should Families Consider When Choosing Accommodation?

Families should look beyond room photographs.

Ask:

  • Are children allowed?
  • What is the minimum age?
  • Are family rooms available?
  • Can rooms connect?
  • Is a private vehicle required?
  • Are babysitting services available?
  • Are swimming pools fenced?
  • Are children allowed on walking activities?
  • Are there child-friendly meals?
  • How far is the lodge from the airstrip?
  • Is there a long transfer after landing?

These details can make a significant difference to the comfort of the trip.

110. What Should Honeymooners Ask Their Lodge About?

Ask about:

  • Private dining
  • Bush dinners
  • Room privacy
  • Plunge pools
  • Spa
  • Honeymoon setup
  • Special activities
  • Photography
  • Private vehicle
  • Airport transfers
  • Beach extensions

Do not assume every "honeymoon package" includes the same things.

111. What Should Photographers Ask Before Booking?

Photographers should ask:

  • Is the vehicle private?
  • How many passengers will it carry?
  • Are windows open?
  • Is the roof open?
  • Is there camera support?
  • Can the vehicle stay longer at sightings?
  • Are low-angle positions possible?
  • Are hides available?
  • Are night drives permitted?
  • Is off-road driving permitted?
  • What is the guide's photography experience?
  • Can the itinerary prioritize sunrise and sunset?

A normal safari and a photographic safari can be completely different experiences.

112. What Should Solo Travelers Ask?

Ask about:

  • Single supplements
  • Shared vehicles
  • Group size
  • Room arrangements
  • Airport transfers
  • Internal flight costs
  • Emergency support

Joining a small group can make safari significantly more affordable for a solo traveler.

113. How Should I Compare Two Safari Quotations?

Do not compare only the total price.

Compare:

  • Accommodation: are both using the same standard?
  • Location: is one property inside a wildlife area while the other is outside?
  • Transport: private vehicle or shared?
  • Guide: dedicated guide or shared?
  • Park fees: included or excluded?
  • Flights: included or excluded?
  • Activities: included or optional?
  • Meals: full board or partial board?
  • Transfers: included or extra?
  • Insurance: included or not?
  • Taxes: included or excluded?

A cheaper quotation may simply contain fewer inclusions.

114. What Questions Should I Ask a Safari Operator Before Paying?

Ask:

  • What exactly is included?
  • What is excluded?
  • Is the vehicle private?
  • Who will guide us?
  • What type of vehicle will we use?
  • Are park fees included?
  • Are internal flights included?
  • What are the luggage limits?
  • What happens if a flight is delayed?
  • What happens if we become sick?
  • What is the cancellation policy?
  • What payment schedule applies?
  • Is emergency evacuation included?
  • What are the visa requirements for our nationality?
  • Are there age restrictions for our children?
  • Are special diets possible?
  • What happens if weather affects the itinerary?

A professional operator should be able to explain these clearly.

115. What Is a Reasonable Safari Cancellation Policy?

Cancellation terms vary by operator and supplier.

Some camps require substantial deposits that become non-refundable close to arrival because the room has been reserved exclusively for you.

Ask for the cancellation policy before paying.

Pay particular attention to:

  • Deposit percentage
  • Cancellation deadlines
  • Refund percentage
  • Non-refundable flights
  • Permit cancellation
  • Peak-season restrictions
  • Insurance requirements

Travel insurance can provide additional protection against certain unexpected circumstances, subject to the policy terms.

116. What Should I Know About Deposits?

A safari deposit is normally used to secure accommodation, transport, permits and other services.

The amount varies by operator.

Before paying, make sure you receive:

  • Written quotation
  • Booking confirmation
  • Payment instructions
  • Terms and conditions
  • Cancellation policy
  • Detailed itinerary

Avoid sending money to unofficial personal accounts unless you have independently verified the company and payment arrangement.

117. How Do I Know if a Safari Company Is Reliable?

Look for:

  • Registered business information
  • Clear contact details
  • Physical office information where applicable
  • Transparent itinerary
  • Clear inclusions
  • Real reviews
  • Professional communication
  • Secure payment procedures
  • Emergency contact
  • Clear cancellation terms
  • Evidence of appropriate tourism licensing where applicable

Do not choose a company solely because it is the cheapest.

118. What Should I Do the Week Before Departure?

One week before:

  • Check passport validity
  • Confirm visa/eTA
  • Confirm flights
  • Check baggage allowance
  • Confirm airport transfer
  • Check travel insurance
  • Check vaccinations
  • Confirm medication
  • Download itinerary

  • Charge power banks
  • Prepare camera equipment

The day before:

  • Keep passport accessible
  • Charge devices
  • Weigh luggage
  • Keep medications in hand luggage
  • Keep travel documents together
  • Confirm departure time
  • Sleep properly

119. What Should I Do When I Arrive?

At the airport:

  • Complete immigration.
  • Collect luggage.
  • Check for missing baggage.
  • Pass customs.
  • Meet your transfer representative.
  • Confirm the next part of your itinerary.
  • Keep important documents accessible.

Do not leave the airport with an unknown person simply because they know your name.

Use the transfer information provided by your operator.

120. What If My Driver Is Not Waiting?

Stay inside the airport or another safe designated area.

Do not immediately arrange a random taxi.

Call:

  • Safari operator
  • Emergency contact
  • Hotel
  • Transfer company

Allow for flight delays and baggage collection before assuming someone has failed to arrive.

121. How Much Free Time Should I Have in a Safari Itinerary?

More than you may think.

A good safari should not be planned minute by minute.

Allow time for:

  • Wildlife sightings
  • Traffic
  • Weather
  • Delays
  • Meals
  • Rest
  • Unexpected discoveries

Wildlife does not follow a timetable.

You may spend ten minutes watching an elephant or an hour watching a pride of lions.

The itinerary should allow room for these moments.

122. What Should I Expect From a Luxury Safari?

Luxury safari is about more than expensive accommodation.

A well-designed luxury safari can provide:

  • Excellent location
  • Privacy
  • Personal service
  • High-quality guiding
  • Comfortable transport
  • Exceptional food
  • Scenic accommodation
  • Flexible activities
  • Carefully coordinated transfers

A luxury room in the wrong location can be less valuable than a simpler camp in a highly productive wildlife area.

123. What Should I Expect From a Budget Safari?

A budget safari can still provide an excellent wildlife experience.

You may simply trade some comfort and convenience for a lower price.

Potential differences include:

  • Basic accommodation
  • Shared transport
  • Longer road transfers
  • Fewer premium facilities
  • Less private space
  • More fixed schedules

The wildlife itself does not become "budget."

You are paying primarily for the logistics surrounding the wildlife experience.

124. Is a More Expensive Safari Always Better?

No.

The right safari is the one that matches your priorities.

A traveler who wants wildlife and adventure may prefer a comfortable mid-range tented camp.

Another traveler may value private pools, gourmet dining and exclusive-use accommodation.

The higher price should provide something that matters to you.

125. What Are the Most Common Safari Planning Mistakes?

  • Trying to see too much: too many parks create unnecessary travel.
  • Choosing only by price: cheap can become expensive when exclusions are added.
  • Ignoring geography: a destination may look close on a map but require a long road journey.
  • Booking the wrong season: your wildlife priorities should influence your dates.
  • Ignoring luggage restrictions: small aircraft have different limits.
  • Leaving visa planning too late: entry requirements can take time.
  • Packing too much: safari travel is easier with less luggage.
  • Expecting wildlife guarantees: nature is unpredictable.
  • Choosing accommodation before choosing the route: the park and season should come first.
  • Booking activities without checking age restrictions: not every experience is suitable for every traveler.
  • Forgetting travel insurance: remote travel requires preparation for unexpected events.

128. Quick Answers to Common Safari Questions

Do I need to be extremely fit?

No for an ordinary vehicle safari. Fitness becomes more important for walking safaris, gorilla trekking and mountain climbs.

Do I need binoculars?

They are highly recommended.

Can I use my phone?

Usually yes, but network coverage varies.

Will I have Wi-Fi?

Often at lodges, but not reliably in remote areas.

Can I use a credit card?

Often, but carry backup cash.

Lodges and city hotels usually take Visa and Mastercard at 2-4 percent over cash; bush camps may take cards for the deposit only and ask for the cash balance on arrival. Mobile money (M-Pesa in Kenya and Tanzania) covers tips and curio stalls where cards do not.

Should I bring a large suitcase?

Not if your itinerary includes small aircraft. Check luggage restrictions.

Can I see the Big Five?

It is possible in several destinations, but never guaranteed.

Can I travel during the rainy season?

Yes. It can be an excellent time to travel depending on your priorities.

Is safari safe?

Safari is generally safe when travelers follow professional guidance and wildlife regulations.

Can children go on safari?

Yes, but age restrictions apply to some activities.

Can I combine safari with Zanzibar?

Yes, particularly with East African itineraries.

Can I customize my safari?

Yes. Route, accommodation, activities, pace and transport can often be customized.

Do I need travel insurance?

Strongly recommended.

Do I need malaria medication?

That is a medical decision based on destination and personal circumstances. Speak with a travel-health professional.

Can I drink alcohol?

Often yes at lodges and camps, but follow property rules and never compromise safety.

Can I take a drone?

Only where permitted. Check aviation, park and property regulations first.

Can I see wildlife from my lodge?

Sometimes. Some lodges are located beside rivers, waterholes or open plains where wildlife can be visible from camp.

129. The Most Important Safari Rule

Do not travel to Africa expecting a controlled wildlife show.

The greatest strength of a safari is precisely the fact that the outcome is not completely controlled.

The animal may appear five minutes after you leave.

The lion may sleep all morning.

The leopard may disappear into tall grass.

The elephants may cross the road directly in front of your vehicle.

A storm may transform the landscape.

A quiet morning may become extraordinary because your guide notices a tiny track in the sand.

The purpose of a good safari is therefore not to guarantee a list of animals.

It is to put you in the right place, at the right time, with enough time and knowledge to appreciate what happens naturally.

130. How to Build the Right African Safari

Start with five questions:

  1. What do you most want to see? Big Five? Migration? Gorillas? Elephants? Predators? Birds? Landscapes?
  2. When can you travel? Your dates can influence wildlife, weather, prices and availability.
  3. How much time do you have? A four-day safari should be designed differently from a two-week journey.
  4. What level of comfort do you want? Choose between Budget, Mid-Range, Luxury and Ultra-Luxury according to what matters to you.
  5. How do you want to travel? Private, small group, road-based, fly-in, walking-focused, photography-focused, family or honeymoon?

Once those five questions are answered, the route becomes much easier to build.

131. Final Safari Planning Advice

The strongest African safari itineraries are not necessarily the longest, most expensive or most complicated.

They are the ones where the season, wildlife, route, accommodation, transport and traveler's priorities work together.

  • Choose the ecosystem before choosing the lodge.
  • Choose the route before adding unnecessary destinations.
  • Choose the season based on what you want to experience.
  • Choose accommodation according to your priorities rather than simply its price.
  • Leave enough time for wildlife.
  • Build realistic transfer days.
  • Check entry requirements before departure.
  • Keep important documents and medication accessible.

And most importantly, allow the safari to surprise you.

Africa is not a destination where every moment can be planned.

That is part of why people return.
