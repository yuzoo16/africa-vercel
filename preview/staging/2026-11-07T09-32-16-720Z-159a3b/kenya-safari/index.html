What is a Kenya safari?

A Kenya safari takes you from the open grasslands of the Masai Mara to elephant country beneath Mount Kilimanjaro, across the immense wilderness of Tsavo and into the dry northern landscapes around Samburu - several completely different safaris inside one small, well-connected country.

It is a short-haul, logistically simple destination. International flights land in Nairobi, most itineraries start within an hour of the airport, and the country's best-known reserves are close enough to combine on a road loop or a couple of short internal flights. That makes Kenya one of the easiest places in Africa to build a first safari, an extended private trip, or a wildlife-and-beach combination around the Indian Ocean coast.

Whether you are planning your first African safari, travelling for the Great Migration, looking specifically for the Big Five, travelling with children, planning a luxury trip or pairing the bush with a beach holiday, Kenya has a route that fits. This page covers the parks, the seasons, the costs, the logistics and the choices that matter, so you can start a conversation with a specialist already knowing what you want.

Why choose a Kenya safari?

Kenya is one of East Africa's most diverse safari destinations, and it is not defined by a single park or a single wildlife experience.

Its protected landscapes range from open savannah and semi-arid wilderness to highland forest, wetlands, Rift Valley lakes and a tropical coastline. That variety is the whole point: you can spend several days following lions and cheetahs across the Mara, watch large elephant herds under Kilimanjaro in Amboseli, search for northern specialist species in Samburu, explore the sheer scale of Tsavo, then finish on the Indian Ocean - without spending the entire trip inside one ecosystem.

Exceptional wildlife viewing

Kenya supports large populations of elephants, lions, buffalo, giraffes, zebras and wildebeest, alongside threatened and highly specialised species that exist almost nowhere else. Amboseli holds more than 400 recorded bird species and some of Africa's most photographed elephants; Tsavo's elephants are famous for the red dust they bathe in; Samburu is the only place in Kenya to see Grevy's zebra, reticulated giraffe, gerenuk, Beisa oryx and Somali ostrich together.

The Masai Mara and the Great Migration

The Masai Mara is part of the larger Serengeti-Mara ecosystem and forms the northern end of the annual wildebeest migration cycle. From July to October the herds concentrate in the Mara and river crossings become possible - though never predictable. Outside those months the reserve still holds resident prides, leopards and cheetahs at densities few places can match. Read the Mara migration guide for month-by-month positioning.

Dramatic landscapes

Kenya's safari scenery changes completely from one destination to the next:

  • Open Mara grasslands with long sightlines
  • Amboseli's plains with Mount Kilimanjaro behind the herds
  • Red-earth wilderness and lava flows in Tsavo
  • Riverine doum-palm country in Samburu
  • Rift Valley lakes and soda shores
  • Highland forest and moorland in the Aberdares
  • True desert and drylands in the north
  • White-sand Indian Ocean beaches

Wildlife and culture together

A Kenyan safari can include time with Maasai and Samburu communities, community-run conservancy projects and a much clearer picture of how people, wildlife and conservation actually fit together. Done well it is the part of the trip travellers remember longest - see cultural tours for how we run them.

Flexible safari styles

Kenya works for first-time safari travellers, couples, families, honeymooners, luxury travellers, photographers, birdwatchers and anyone short on time. It suits short breaks of two or three days, classic week-long loops, and extended East African safaris that cross into Tanzania. Most of that flexibility comes down to choosing between the private, luxury, family and honeymoon formats described further down this page.

Kenya's main safari destinations

Kenya's protected areas are not one landscape with different names. Each one gives you a genuinely different safari, and the order you visit them changes the trip more than the number of days does.

Four destinations carry most first-time itineraries. The Masai Mara delivers predator density and, in season, the Migration. Amboseli is an elephant and Kilimanjaro destination. Samburu replaces the classic southern species with northern specialists you will not see elsewhere in Kenya. Tsavo is where you go for scale - more wilderness than any itinerary can use. Masai Mara National Reserve

The Masai Mara is the destination most closely associated with a Kenya safari, and for good reason - it holds the highest concentration of large mammals in the country inside an area you can cross in an afternoon.

Located in south-western Kenya, the Mara forms the northern extension of the Serengeti ecosystem. It is known for open grasslands, high concentrations of large mammals and excellent predator viewing: resident prides of lions, leopards in the riverine forest, cheetahs on the short-grass plains and hyena clans that have been studied longer than almost any others in Africa.

Resident wildlife is present all year. The annual migration brings huge concentrations of wildebeest and zebra into the Mara for part of the year, and the river crossings that follow are the most photographed events in African safari travel - though they are never scheduled and never guaranteed. See the Migration in the Mara for how the season actually moves.

The Mara is particularly attractive to travellers interested in lions, leopards, cheetahs, elephants, buffalo, wildebeest, zebra, giraffes, hyenas, hippos, crocodiles, birdlife, the Great Migration and wildlife photography - which is most of them.

The surrounding conservancies offer a different style of safari from the main reserve. Bed numbers are limited, off-road driving and night drives are permitted, and vehicles per sighting are capped. Accommodation, community conservation and permitted activities vary by individual conservancy, and that difference matters more than the star rating on the tent. For a short version, see the 3-day Mara safari or the Masai Mara safari hub.

Amboseli National Park

Amboseli is one of Kenya's most recognisable safari landscapes - flat open plains, swamps fed by underground springs from Kilimanjaro, and some of the last great-tusker elephants in Africa.

The park's water table sits low and its swamps stay green through the dry season, which concentrates wildlife against a backdrop of Mount Kilimanjaro. Views toward the mountain are clearest at dawn in the drier months, so Amboseli is a park where the alarm call is worth taking.

Amboseli is especially suited to travellers looking for elephants, Kilimanjaro scenery, photography, big-game viewing, birdwatching, short safari extensions from Nairobi and cultural experiences with the surrounding Maasai communities. It records more than 400 bird species across its swamps, plains and acacia woodland, and it pairs naturally before a Mara fly-in rather than after.

Tsavo East National Park

Tsavo West and Tsavo East together form the largest single wildlife area in Kenya. Tsavo East alone covers approximately 13,747 sq km - larger than many countries on most safari itineraries.

Its semi-arid landscapes, open plains, acacia bushland, rivers and volcanic scenery create a completely different atmosphere from the Mara. This is the park of distance and silence rather than crowded sightings, and the elephants here are famous for the red dust they wallow in.

Highlights include red-dusted elephants, lions, buffalo, giraffes, zebras, leopards, the Galana River, the Yatta Plateau, Lugard's Falls, Aruba Dam, superb birdwatching and serious photographic space. Tsavo East also offers more than conventional daytime game drives: guided walks, photography-focused experiences and selected night and astronomy experiences run in the wider Tsavo area.

Tsavo West National Park

Tsavo West is the greener, more dramatic half of the Tsavo ecosystem - volcanic terrain, lava flows, hills, springs and hidden oases.

Its best-known attraction is Mzima Springs, where clear water surfaces through volcanic rock and supports a concentration of wildlife and birdlife; hippos and crocodiles hold the pools while elephants come down to drink. The Shetani Lava Flow, Lake Jipe, rhino conservation areas and a string of scenic viewpoints complete the picture.

Highlights include Mzima Springs, the Shetani Lava Flow, rhino conservation, elephants, lions, buffalo, leopards, birdlife, Lake Jipe and volcanic landscapes. Tsavo West covers approximately 9,065 sq km and, with Tsavo East, forms part of the wider Tsavo Conservation Area - so a combined Tsavo itinerary is really one safari across a vast single ecosystem.

Samburu National Reserve

Samburu introduces a completely different landscape to the classic southern Kenya safari: dry plains, rocky ground, doum palms and riverine habitat along the Ewaso Nyiro River in the north of the country.

It is famous for the so-called Samburu Special Five - species adapted to arid northern country that do not occur in the Mara or Amboseli:

  • Grevy's zebra
  • Reticulated giraffe
  • Gerenuk
  • Beisa oryx
  • Somali ostrich

Samburu also supports lions, leopards - including some well-known resident individuals - cheetahs, elephants, buffalo and an enormous variety of birdlife. The riverine landscape is the reason: predators hold the green corridor while the plains outside it empty out. A Samburu safari pairs well with cultural visits to local communities, and it adds a genuinely different ecosystem to a route that would otherwise be all southern Kenya. See the Samburu reserve guide.

Lake Nakuru National Park

Lake Nakuru is a compact park that combines lakeshore scenery, woodland, grassland and some of the most reliable rhino viewing in Kenya.

The park is important for rhinoceros conservation and holds both black and white rhino inside a fenced, well-managed area - which is why Nakuru features on so many first safari itineraries. The alkaline lake and the surrounding habitats also attract diverse birdlife, and the wooded ridges around the shore support lions, leopards, buffalo, giraffes, zebras and antelope.

Commonly associated wildlife includes black rhino, white rhino, lions, leopards, buffalo, giraffes, zebras, antelope and large numbers of water-associated bird species. For bird-focused routing, see birdwatching safaris.

Nairobi National Park

A Kenya safari can begin almost immediately after you land. Nairobi National Park sits on the southern edge of the capital, which produces the strangest photograph in African safari travel: wildlife against a city skyline.

The park holds more than 100 animal species and more than 400 bird species, and it is particularly important for rhino conservation. Because it is minutes from the airport, it works well as a first safari before travelling onward, a short game drive on a tight schedule, a final drive before an international flight, a photography session, or a Nairobi stopover activity.

Allow real margin for traffic when you build it around a departure - Nairobi's roads, not the park, are the variable.

Meru National Park

Meru offers a more secluded wilderness experience in eastern Kenya, and it is the least visited park on this page - which is exactly its appeal.

The park combines grasslands, bushland, riverine environments and wildlife corridors, with a conservation history tied to George and Joy Adamson and Elsa the lioness. It supports elephants, lions, leopards, buffalo, rhinos, cheetahs, Grevy's zebra, reticulated giraffe and diverse birdlife, and it holds a dedicated rhino sanctuary.

Meru also runs wildlife viewing, nature experiences and night or astronomy tourism. It suits travellers who have already seen the northern circuit and want somewhere with fewer vehicles and more range.

Aberdare National Park

For travellers who want something beyond classic savannah, the Aberdare region offers montane forest, moorland, waterfalls, mountain scenery and genuinely cool highland conditions.

The landscape is home to elephants, buffalo and other forest-adapted wildlife, and the altitude changes what a safari feels like: mist in the morning, cold at night, and viewpoints over the Rift Valley. Highlights include mountain scenery, montane forest, moorlands, waterfalls, hiking, scenic viewpoints, wildlife viewing and the historic Treetops area, where a visiting princess learned she had become queen.

The Aberdares are a strong contrast to Kenya's warmer open plains, and they work well as a stop between the Rift Valley lakes and Mount Kenya country.

Kenya's safari ecosystems

One reason Kenya works so well for safari planning is the range of ecosystems available inside a single country - often within a few hours of each other.

The Mara grasslands

Open grassland gives long viewing distances, ideal habitat for grazing herds and the predators that follow them. This is the landscape most people picture when they picture Kenya.

Amboseli wetlands and plains

Seasonal and permanent water sources hold wildlife through dry country, with elephants as the area's signature attraction and Kilimanjaro supplying the backdrop.

Tsavo's semi-arid wilderness

Enormous scale, volcanic scenery, dry bushland, rivers and wildlife adapted to harsh conditions - a safari built around space rather than sightings per hour.

Northern Kenya

Samburu and the surrounding landscapes support species specifically adapted to dry northern environments, several of which exist nowhere else in Kenya.

Rift Valley lakes

Lake Nakuru and the other Rift Valley destinations combine water, birdlife and terrestrial wildlife in a compact area that is easy to route between parks.

Highland forests

The Aberdare and Mount Kenya landscapes introduce forest, high elevation and cooler conditions, plus hiking and walking that a plain-based safari cannot offer.

Coastal and marine ecosystems

Kenya's Indian Ocean coast adds beaches, coral reefs, marine parks and water-based activities to an itinerary - the reason safari and beach combinations are so popular here.

Kenya Big Five safari

A Big Five safari focuses on the five animals traditionally used to describe Africa's most famous large mammals: lion, leopard, African elephant, African buffalo and rhinoceros.

The Big Five are not a guarantee or a checklist. Wildlife is free-ranging, and sightings vary with location, season, weather, habitat and luck. Any operator who promises all five on fixed days is guessing.

What Kenya can offer is range. The Masai Mara, Lake Nakuru, Amboseli, Tsavo and Meru can all contribute to a Big Five-focused itinerary, but the experience and the likelihood of seeing each species differs between them. If rhino is your priority, Lake Nakuru and the specialist conservancies are where to concentrate; if elephants are the point, Amboseli and Tsavo give you two completely different versions of the same animal. Our private safaris can be built around whichever of those matters most to you.

The Great Migration in Kenya

The Great Migration is one of the major reasons travellers plan a Kenya safari - and one of the most misunderstood.

It is not a single event on a fixed date. It is a continuous ecological movement across the Serengeti-Mara ecosystem, driven by rainfall, grass condition, water availability and animal behaviour. The Masai Mara is the northern part of that wider system, and it receives the herds for part of the year rather than on a published schedule.

When the migrating herds reach the Mara, visitors may encounter huge wildebeest concentrations, zebra, predators tracking the herds, crocodiles and hippos along the rivers, dramatic crossing scenes and visibly increased activity on the open plains.

River crossings are highly unpredictable. Animals may gather for hours or days before crossing, or move away without crossing at all, and no guide can schedule one for you. The useful planning advice is to position yourself in the right part of the ecosystem for the right weeks and let the animals decide. Start with when to go for the Migration and the Mara river crossing guide.

Kenya safari for different travellers

The right Kenya safari depends less on the country's most famous reserve and more on who is travelling and what they want the trip to feel like.

For a first-time safari

Kenya is one of the easiest places in Africa to start. Nairobi is a straightforward arrival, the Mara and Amboseli are short hops or a manageable road loop away, English is widely spoken, and the accommodation range is enormous. A first safari usually wants one predator-rich reserve, one contrasting landscape and enough nights in each to stop feeling like a transit. Read planning your first safari before you book anything.

For families

Family safaris work when the itinerary is built around the children's ages rather than around a checklist of parks. Shorter driving days, lodges with pools, camps that take children in the room, and a private vehicle so you can stop when someone spots a chameleon - those matter more than adding a fourth park. Lake Naivasha boats and Crescent Island walking break up drive days nicely, and fly-ins skip the long roads that tire younger travellers. See Kenya family safaris and our family safari formats.

For honeymoons

A honeymoon itinerary usually combines wildlife with privacy: a conservancy camp with few vehicles, sundowners, bush dinners, a scenic flight and then the Indian Ocean. It works best when the beach comes second and the bush comes first, while the energy is highest. See Kenya honeymoon safaris.

For photographers

Photography is the one interest that changes vehicle logistics. You want flexible game drives, longer stays in fewer places, guides who understand light, and ideally a private vehicle so a sighting can hold for an hour instead of ten minutes. See Kenya photographic safaris and our safari photography guide.

For luxury and private travellers

Luxury in Kenya is mostly about access - private conservancies, exclusive-use vehicles, fly-in routing and camps with a real relationship with the land around them. Compare luxury Kenya safaris with private safaris, and note that price differences between operators are usually accommodation and vehicle, not wildlife quality.

Best time to visit Kenya

There is no single best month for every Kenya safari. The ideal period depends on what you want to see, where you are travelling, and whether you prioritise dry conditions, Migration activity, photography, birding or lower-season value.

June to October

This is Kenya's dry season and the period most associated with classic safari conditions: easier wildlife viewing, drier roads, animals concentrated around water, strong photographic light, and Migration activity in the Mara depending on the year. It is also peak season, so parks and conservancies are busiest and prices are at their highest.

November to December

The short rains transform the landscape. Expect green scenery, fewer visitors in some areas, young wildlife, excellent photography and migratory bird activity. Rain can affect road conditions and the shape of a daily game drive, so build in flexibility.

January to February

Often relatively dry across much of the country, with clear mornings and good wildlife viewing - and one of the best windows for photographs of Kilimanjaro from Amboseli. A strong value period outside the European school holidays.

March to May

The long rains bring greener landscapes and fewer visitors. This period suits travellers who value green scenery, birding, photography and a quieter atmosphere, but heavy rainfall can affect roads and access in some regions - we route around the worst of it or shift dates rather than run you through it.

Kenya safari cost

The cost of a Kenya safari depends on several variables rather than one fixed national price, and the single biggest driver is never the park - it is where you sleep.

The factors that move the number: number of safari days, number of travellers, private or shared vehicle, accommodation category, park and reserve fees, domestic flights, road transfers, guide services, season, activities, beach extension, special experiences and the level of luxury you choose. See the full African safari cost guide for how each one is priced.

Budget Kenya safari

Budget safaris generally use simpler accommodation and shared or cost-efficient transport. They work well for travellers who would rather spend their money on days in the parks than on rooms - and they are honest about it.

Mid-range Kenya safari

Mid-range safaris balance comfortable lodges and tented camps with private or shared safari arrangements. This is the practical option for most couples, families and first-time travellers, and where the bulk of our bookings sit.

Luxury Kenya safari

Luxury safaris can include premium tented camps, luxury lodges, private vehicles and guides, scenic flights, exclusive conservancy access, private dining and fully tailored days. What you are buying is space, flexibility and access rather than better wildlife. See the luxury safari guide.

Why Kenya safari prices vary

Two safaris visiting the same park can cost very different amounts, because accommodation, transport, vehicle type and inclusions change the total far more than the wildlife does. That is why a quote should always show park fees and conservancy fees as line items - if they are hidden inside a headline price, you cannot compare operators honestly.

Kenya safari and beach

One of Kenya's strongest itinerary combinations is wildlife first, Indian Ocean coast second - and the logistics are simpler than most people expect.

A typical journey runs Nairobi → Masai Mara → Amboseli → Diani Beach, or Nairobi → Tsavo → Diani, or Nairobi → Samburu → Masai Mara → Diani. Direct flights from the Mara strip to the coast mean you do not have to backtrack through Nairobi, which is the difference between a good trip and an exhausting one.

Beach extensions commonly use Diani, Mombasa, Watamu, Malindi and Lamu. Wildlife and marine tourism are treated as complementary halves of the same holiday rather than competing choices - and three beach nights after seven safari days is the formula most people come back to. See Kenya safari and beach.

Diani

The default finish: long white sand, warm water, easy flights from the Mara. Relaxation, swimming, water sports, diving and snorkelling.

Watamu

Reef, marine park and a quieter atmosphere - the better choice if you would rather snorkel than sunbathe.

Mombasa

Beaches alongside history, food, culture and coastal heritage - the most layered of the three.

Kenya safari activities

Game drives are the foundation of every Kenya safari, but they are not the whole trip.

Game drives

Early morning drives are usually the most productive: temperatures are cooler, predators are active and the light is good. Afternoon drives give you a second window on different habitats and changing animal behaviour. See game drives.

Full-day game drives

A full day out covers a much larger area without repeatedly returning to camp - especially useful in parks as big as Tsavo, where the distance between sightings is the whole point.

Walking safaris

Walking gives you a closer understanding of tracks, plants, insects, birds and smaller wildlife that a vehicle drives straight past. It must always follow local rules and be conducted in appropriate areas with qualified staff. See walking safaris.

Hot-air balloon safaris

Balloon flights run in selected destinations, most famously over the Mara. They give you the landscape from the air and are normally followed by a bush breakfast on the ground. Worth doing once. See balloon safaris.

Night and astronomy experiences

Selected Kenyan parks run night wildlife and astronomy experiences - Amboseli, Tsavo East, Tsavo West and Meru among them. They are permitted in specific areas only, never a general activity. See night safaris.

Birdwatching

Kenya is an outstanding birding destination because its habitats are so varied. Potential highlights include raptors, waterbirds, migratory species, savannah birds, forest species and dryland specialists - with Amboseli alone recording more than 400 species. See birdwatching safaris.

Cultural experiences

Depending on the itinerary you may spend time with Maasai or Samburu communities. A good cultural experience centres on respectful interaction and learning rather than treating communities as entertainment. See cultural tours.

Hiking and adventure

Kenya also offers hiking and mountain experiences in the Mount Kenya region, the Rift Valley and selected highland destinations - a useful counterpoint to long days in a vehicle.

Kenya safari accommodation

Accommodation is usually the largest single line in a Kenya safari budget, and the choice matters more than the star rating suggests.

Safari lodges

Permanent lodges offer rooms or suites, restaurants, swimming pools, common areas and established facilities. They are the easiest option for families and travellers who want reliability over atmosphere.

Tented camps

Tented camps run from comfortable mid-range properties to highly polished mobile-style camps. Canvas, insects at night and the sound of the bush create a much stronger sense of being in the landscape than a concrete room does.

Luxury safari camps

The top end provides large private tents, en-suite facilities, private decks, butler-style service, private dining, spa treatments and exclusive activities - usually in a private conservancy where vehicle numbers are controlled.

Community and conservancy camps

Around the Masai Mara and Samburu, conservancy-based accommodation gives you a different relationship between tourism, wildlife and local communities: limited beds, community revenue and access to areas the main reserve does not allow. See Kenya accommodation.

Choosing where to stay

Do not choose accommodation by looking at the room. Look at location first:

  • Distance to park gates and wildlife areas
  • Driving time from the previous night's camp
  • Conservancy access and permitted activities
  • Road conditions in that season
  • Room configuration and child policies
  • Meal arrangements and dietary flexibility

In the Masai Mara in particular, where you sleep decides how much of the ecosystem you can actually reach - that single choice changes a seven-day itinerary more than any other.

Getting around Kenya on safari

Kenya safaris run by road, by air, or - most often - by a combination of both.

4x4 safari vehicles

A proper 4x4 matters for game viewing, photography, rougher road sections, flexible road itineraries and private safaris. Ask what you are actually getting: pop-up roof or sliding windows, fridge, charging points and how many people are sharing the vehicle all change the experience.

Domestic flights

Flying between safari destinations can remove entire days of road travel. It is the standard way to link distant regions such as Samburu → Masai Mara or Masai Mara → Diani, and it is what makes a seven-day Kenya safari possible without seven days of driving.

Road safaris

Road travel lets you see more of the Kenyan countryside between destinations and is often more economical. It works best when the destinations are genuinely close and you enjoy the journey rather than just the arrival.

Road or flying?

Choose road travel when you enjoy scenic journeys, destinations are relatively close, you want to control costs, or you want to see more of the country between parks. Consider flying when distances are large, time is limited, you want more hours inside the parks, or your route includes remote northern or coastal destinations.

Kenya safari gateways

Nairobi is the main starting point, convenient for the Masai Mara, Lake Nakuru, Amboseli, Samburu, Nairobi National Park and any multi-park itinerary. Jomo Kenyatta International Airport handles international arrivals and departures. Wilson Airport is the one that matters for domestic safari flights - the Mara hop is around 45 minutes. Mombasa works for travellers combining the coast with Tsavo or the south-east.

How many days do you need for a Kenya safari?

The right length depends on how many ecosystems you want and how fast you are willing to move between them.

2-3 days

Suitable for a short Masai Mara safari, Nairobi plus the Mara, a short Amboseli extension, or a safari stopover between other travel.

4-5 days

A good length for combining two destinations - Lake Nakuru + Masai Mara, Amboseli + Tsavo, or Masai Mara + one nearby destination.

6-8 days

Enough time for the Masai Mara, Lake Nakuru, Amboseli, Samburu and Tsavo in some combination, depending on route and transport. This is where flying starts paying for itself.

9-14 days

A longer Kenya safari can cover several ecosystems without turning every day into a transfer day. Both of these work:

Nairobi → Samburu → Lake Nakuru → Masai Mara → Amboseli → Nairobi

Nairobi → Masai Mara → Amboseli → Tsavo → Diani Beach

Kenya and Tanzania safari

Kenya and Tanzania combine into one East African safari, and for travellers interested in the wider Serengeti-Mara ecosystem it is the best possible version of both.

The Mara and the Serengeti are not two separate attractions - they are the two halves of one ecological system, and crossing the border lets you follow it rather than sample it. A longer itinerary can run Nairobi → Masai Mara → Serengeti → Ngorongoro → Tarangire → Arusha, or Masai Mara → Serengeti → Ngorongoro → Zanzibar if you want to finish in the water.

The two countries offer different protected areas and different logistics, while sharing that same ecosystem. Cross-border trips need two park fee systems, border formalities and a longer itinerary than either country alone - which is why they are worth planning as a single route from the start. Photography and birdwatching in Kenya

Two interests shape a Kenya itinerary more than any others, and both reward a slower pace over a longer list of parks.

Photography

Kenya provides an exceptional range of subjects. For wildlife: lions, leopards, cheetahs, elephants, rhinos, buffalo, giraffes, wildebeest, zebras and birds. For landscapes: the Mara plains, Kilimanjaro from Amboseli, the Tsavo wilderness, the Rift Valley, Samburu's riverine country, highland forest and the coast.

Two practical rules make most of the difference. Use the early and the late light whenever possible - the middle of the day is photographically dead and the animals think so too. And allow enough time in one destination instead of trying to photograph everything from a moving vehicle: a private safari is genuinely more valuable here, because the vehicle can stay with a sighting without having to accommodate other guests. See photographic safaris.

Birdwatching

Kenya's varied habitats support a remarkable range of species. Amboseli records more than 400 bird species and Nairobi National Park more than 400 as well, while Tsavo East and Tsavo West add opportunities across semi-arid and riverine country.

A bird-focused itinerary might combine Amboseli, Lake Nakuru, the Masai Mara, Samburu, Tsavo and the Aberdares - but the best destination depends entirely on the species and habitat you want to target, so tell us what is on your list before we build the route.

Responsible Kenya safari

A responsible safari protects wildlife while respecting the people and landscapes that make it possible.

Follow park rules

Stay in designated areas, respect wildlife, do not feed animals, stay on established roads and keep an appropriate viewing distance. Park rules include a maximum speed of 40 km/h, a minimum 20-metre wildlife viewing distance, restrictions on off-road driving and a prohibition on feeding wildlife - and they are enforced because they work.

Avoid single-use plastics

Single-use plastics are restricted in Kenya's national parks and reserves. Pack accordingly and carry a reusable bottle rather than buying water at every stop.

Support conservation

Choose operators and accommodation that contribute meaningfully to wildlife conservation, local employment, community development, habitat protection and responsible tourism. Ask what a camp does, not just what it looks like.

Respect local communities

When visiting Maasai or Samburu communities: ask before taking photographs, respect local customs, listen and learn, buy crafts directly where appropriate, and do not reduce a community to a stereotype for the sake of a picture.

Give wildlife space

A good safari is not measured by how close a vehicle gets to an animal. Give wildlife room and let natural behaviour continue - the sighting is better for everyone when the animal decides what happens next.

Before you travel: entry, health and packing

Three things are worth settling before you book flights: your entry documents, a conversation with a travel-health professional, and what goes in the bag.

Entry requirements

Kenya operates an Electronic Travel Authorisation (eTA) system. Visitors who require an eTA should obtain approval before travelling, while citizens of certain East African Partner States and other listed categories are exempt. Requirements change, so verify your own status directly with Kenya's immigration authorities before departure.

For travellers who do need an eTA, the current application guidance asks for a passport valid for at least six months, a blank page, a photograph, contact details, and your travel itinerary and accommodation information. We send a step-by-step checklist with every booking, and our safari visa guide covers the regional picture.

Health and safety

Before travelling, speak to a qualified travel-health professional about recommended vaccinations, malaria prevention, travel insurance, any existing medical conditions, prescription medication, and food and water precautions. Conditions differ between regions, so advice should be based on your specific itinerary rather than a general list.

On safari, the practical rules are simple. Follow your guide's instructions. Remain inside the vehicle unless your guide confirms you are in a designated safe area. Do not approach wild animals on foot. Do not feed them. Keep food stored appropriately at camp, use insect repellent and sun protection, and carry enough drinking water. See safari safety for the full picture.

What to pack

Clothing: lightweight, comfortable pieces in neutral or muted colours - lightweight trousers, shirts or T-shirts, a warm layer, a light jacket, comfortable shoes, sandals, a hat, and swimwear if your camp has a pool or you are continuing to the coast.

Safari essentials: binoculars, a camera, spare batteries, memory cards, a power bank, sunglasses, sunscreen, insect repellent, a reusable water bottle, a small day bag and personal medication.

Highland destinations such as the Aberdares get genuinely cold at night, so add warmer clothing for those legs. Our safari packing list has the printable version.

How to choose the right Kenya safari

Start with the experience you want rather than the number of parks you can fit into one itinerary. Almost every route decision follows from the first answer.

If you want the Great Migration

Choose the Masai Mara - timed to the seasonal movement, not a fixed date.

If you want elephants and Kilimanjaro

Choose Amboseli, ideally in the January to March window when the mountain is clearest.

If you want huge wilderness landscapes

Choose Tsavo - East or West, or both.

If you want northern specialist species

Choose Samburu for Grevy's zebra, reticulated giraffe, gerenuk, Beisa oryx and Somali ostrich.

If you want rhinos and Rift Valley scenery

Consider Lake Nakuru.

If you want a safari close to Nairobi

Consider Nairobi National Park - ideal on an arrival or departure day.

If you want forests and mountain landscapes

Consider the Aberdare or Mount Kenya region.

If you want wildlife plus a beach holiday

Consider Kenya safari + Diani, Mombasa, Watamu or Malindi, with the coast at the end of the route rather than the middle.

How to plan your Kenya safari

Nine questions, answered honestly, will tell you more about the right trip than any amount of browsing.

1. How many days do you have?

This determines how many destinations can realistically be included - and it is the first constraint worth being honest about.

2. What wildlife matters most?

Are you here primarily for big cats, elephants, rhinos, the Migration, birds or northern specialist species? The answer changes the route, not just the wish list.

3. What season are you travelling?

Your dates influence landscapes, road conditions, wildlife distribution and accommodation demand. Flexible dates are worth more than a longer itinerary.

4. Do you prefer private or shared travel?

Private safaris give you greater flexibility over departure times, game-drive length, photography stops, route, pace and who else is in the vehicle.

5. Road travel or flights?

Long distances are usually better handled by air; shorter ones are more enjoyable and more economical by road.

6. What accommodation level do you want?

Choose between budget, mid-range, luxury and ultra-luxury according to your priorities - and remember that the wildlife does not change with the thread count.

7. Are you travelling with children?

Check accommodation and activity age policies before confirming anything. See the family safari guide.

8. Do you want the beach?

If yes, build the coastal extension into the route from the beginning - not as an afterthought added at the end.

9. Kenya only, or Kenya and Tanzania?

A cross-border safari needs additional planning and a longer itinerary, but it is the only way to follow the whole Serengeti-Mara system.

Frequently asked questions

When is the best time for a Kenya safari?

July to October for dry-season viewing and Migration activity in the Mara; January to March for resident predators, clear Kilimanjaro views from Amboseli and better value. The April to May long rains are the one window we would plan around rather than through.

Is Kenya good for a first safari?

Yes, and it is one of the best. Nairobi is a simple arrival, the Mara and Amboseli are a short flight or an easy road loop, English is widely spoken and the accommodation range covers every budget. A first trip usually wants two contrasting parks with enough nights in each to stop feeling like a transit.

When is the Great Migration in Kenya?

The herds reach the Masai Mara for part of the year, but the timing changes annually with rainfall, grass condition and water. River crossings cannot be guaranteed on a specific date - plan around the seasonal movement and let the animals decide the rest.

Masai Mara or Serengeti?

Same ecosystem, different staging: the Mara concentrates wildlife and crossings into July to October in a compact area; the Serengeti holds herds for most of the year with calving and Grumeti crossings. Read the full comparison, then choose by month.

How many days do we need in Kenya?

Three days works for the Mara alone as a fly-in. Five to seven covers Amboseli, the lakes and the Mara. Ten adds Samburu or Diani beach. Three Mara nights is the minimum we would recommend - cats and crossings both reward patience.

Can I see the Big Five in Kenya?

Yes. The Mara, Lake Nakuru, Amboseli, Tsavo and Meru all hold lion, leopard, elephant, buffalo and rhino in some combination. What no safari can promise is every species on every trip - wildlife is free-ranging and sightings depend on season, habitat and luck.

Which is better for elephants: Amboseli or Tsavo?

They are different experiences rather than a ranking. Amboseli is elephants against Kilimanjaro - concentrated, photographable, famous for its great tuskers. Tsavo is elephants inside a far larger semi-arid wilderness, where you earn the sighting over distance.

Reserve or conservancy in the Mara?

The national reserve costs less and has more vehicles at sightings. Private conservancies such as Mara North and Naboisho limit bed numbers and allow off-road driving, night drives and walking. Photographers and honeymooners should choose a conservancy.

Is the Masai Mara worth visiting outside Migration season?

Yes. The Mara holds resident prides, leopards and cheetahs year round and is a major destination in every month. The Migration is an additional seasonal event, not the only reason to go - and outside peak season you get fewer vehicles at every sighting.

Is Amboseli worth two days?

Yes, particularly for elephant lovers and photographers: more than 400 elephants including great tuskers, with Kilimanjaro clearest at dawn from January to March. It pairs perfectly before a Mara fly-in.

Is Samburu worth adding to a Kenya safari?

It adds a completely different ecosystem and five northern species you will not see anywhere else in Kenya - Grevy's zebra, reticulated giraffe, gerenuk, Beisa oryx and Somali ostrich. On a route that would otherwise be all southern Kenya, it is the most interesting detour you can take.

Is Lake Nakuru worth visiting?

Especially if rhinos, birdlife or Rift Valley scenery are on your list. It is a compact, well-managed park with both black and white rhino, which makes it one of the most reliable stops on a first itinerary.

How do Nairobi logistics work?

International arrivals land at Jomo Kenyatta. Safari flights leave from Wilson Airport - the Mara hop is around 45 minutes - or you can route by road. Overnight in Nairobi on arrival day, and the Sheldrick elephant orphanage fills a layover beautifully.

Can I visit Nairobi National Park before an international flight?

Yes. Its position minutes from the city makes it possible to include on certain arrival or departure itineraries - but build in real margin for airport procedures and traffic, because the park is not what will make you late.

Is Kenya good for families?

Excellent, when the itinerary is built around the children's ages. Naivasha boats and Crescent Island walks break up drive days, Mara fly-ins skip long roads, and lodges run strong kids' programmes. Under-fives do best with a private vehicle and regular pool stops.

Can I combine a Kenya safari with a beach holiday?

Yes - and it is one of the country's strongest combinations. The Masai Mara, Amboseli or Tsavo all link easily to the Indian Ocean coast by direct flight, so there is no need to backtrack through Nairobi.

How do we add Diani Beach?

Fly the Mara to Diani via Wilson and Ukunda rather than backtracking through Nairobi. Three beach nights minimum, with Wasini dhow trips and Colobus sanctuary walks if you want more than a sun lounger. Seven safari days plus three beach nights is the formula.

Can I combine Kenya and Tanzania?

Yes. The Mara and the Serengeti are two halves of one ecosystem, so a cross-border route follows the landscape rather than doubling back. It needs two park fee systems, border formalities and a longer itinerary - plan it as one trip from the start.

Is Samburu far from the other parks?

It is in the north, so it is either a domestic flight or a long road day from the Mara. That distance is exactly what makes it worth it - the ecosystem and the species are unlike anything in the south.

Is Kenya safe for safari?

Safari destinations operate under established wildlife-management rules, but wild animals remain unpredictable. Follow your guide's instructions, stay in the vehicle where required, keep the required viewing distance and follow park regulations. Beyond the parks, standard travel sense applies - we brief you before departure.

Do I need a visa for Kenya?

Kenya uses an Electronic Travel Authorisation system for many visitors, with exemptions for certain nationalities and categories. Requirements change, so check the official immigration requirements for your passport before you travel.

Since 1 January 2024 Kenya has no visas, only the eTA. It costs US$32.50, is filed online between 90 and three days before travel, needs a hotel booking or invitation showing where you stay, and covers a single entry - you may still cross into Rwanda, Uganda or Tanzania and re-enter while it stays valid. South Africa, Ethiopia, Namibia, Botswana, Singapore and all EAC citizens are exempt outright, and anyone arriving from a yellow fever country must carry the certificate. Approval arrives by email: print it next to your onward ticket, because officers at the counter still want paper.

What should I wear on safari?

Lightweight, comfortable clothing in neutral or muted colours, a warm layer, closed shoes, a hat and sun protection. Highland destinations such as the Aberdares need genuinely warm layers for the evenings.

What does a Kenya safari cost?

Mid-range private loops run from around 350 to 600 dollars per person per day; luxury conservancy camping is 800 and above. Park and conservancy fees should appear as line items in your quote rather than inside a headline price - always confirm that when comparing operators.

Mid-range private loops run from around US$350 to 600 per person per day; luxury conservancy camping is US$800 and above. Kenya's line items differ from Tanzania: conservancy fees (the Maasai Mara conservancies charge around US$100 per adult per day in high season) sit beside park fees, and most circuits add a light-aircraft hop of US$150-300 per sector.

Can I do a Kenya safari on a budget?

Yes. Costs come down with shorter itineraries, simpler accommodation, shared transport and careful destination selection. What you should not compress is the number of nights in the Mara - that is the part you came for.

Yes. A Kenya safari on a budget usually means shorter routes, simpler camps, shared transport and careful timing: conservancy green-season tariffs run 20-40 percent below peak, and public KWS parks like Amboseli undercut private conservancies. What keeps the budget honest is cutting nights, not guiding - a four-night Tsavo-Amboseli loop by road costs less than two nights in the Mara by air, and both show elephants.

Is a private Kenya safari worth considering?

For most travellers, yes. A private safari gives you control over departure times, game-drive length, photography stops, route, accommodation and the overall pace of the trip - which is why it is our default recommendation for families, photographers and anyone with a specific goal.
