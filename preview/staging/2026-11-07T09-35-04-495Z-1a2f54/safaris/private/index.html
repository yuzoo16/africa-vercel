Private Safaris in Africa: Your Own Vehicle, Your Guide, Your Pace

A private safari puts your own vehicle, your own driver-guide and your own schedule at the centre of the trip, so the days follow your interests rather than a fixed timetable.

What a Private Safari Means

A private safari is one where the vehicle, the driver-guide and the day's schedule belong to your group alone. No other travellers are placed in the vehicle with you, and the route is not fixed in advance by a shared timetable. When elephants linger at a crossing for forty minutes, you stay. When a photographer in the vehicle wants to work one sighting slowly, that is what happens. When someone would rather return to camp for a late breakfast, the day reshapes around it.

This is the default style at Adventure Safari Africa rather than an upgrade. Every itinerary we operate runs with a private 4x4 and a dedicated driver-guide, because the difference it makes to a safari is larger than almost any other line item on the budget.

The claim is easy to make and worth testing. On a shared vehicle, the guide drives a compromise route and works to a shared departure time. On a private vehicle, the guide works to your interests, which usually means more time at fewer sightings, fewer rushed arrivals and a noticeably calmer day.

What "Private" Actually Includes

The word gets used loosely across the industry, so it helps to be precise about what you are buying.

A private vehicle: A 4x4 carrying only your group, with a pop-up roof or open sides for viewing, a cooler for drinks and space for camera kit.

A dedicated driver-guide: One guide who stays with you for the duration of the safari rather than being swapped between vehicles at each park.

Your own schedule: Departure times, drive lengths, meal times and rest breaks agreed between you and the guide each evening.

A route built around you: Park sequence and overnight stops chosen for your interests and pace, not to fill a fixed departure.

Two things a private safari does not automatically include. It does not mean off-road driving, which depends on the concession rather than on the vehicle. And it does not mean unlimited driving hours, since park regulations set curfews and guides work licensed shifts.

How a Private Vehicle Changes the Day

The practical difference shows up in small decisions that repeat all day.

The guide can reposition. If a leopard was seen near a waterhole at first light, a private vehicle can drive straight there rather than following a circular route designed to give everyone a bit of everything. If a sighting turns quiet, the guide can leave early and find something better instead of waiting for the slowest member of a shared group.

The guide can wait. Long waits are often what produce the best photographs and the most memorable sightings. A private vehicle can sit with a hunting pride, a nesting pair of rollers or a herd working its way to water, without anyone becoming restless.

The guide can adapt to the group. Photographers typically want low angles, longer stops and fewer interruptions. Families with younger children usually want shorter drives, regular breaks and a guide who explains what they are looking at. Birders want to stop for small brown birds that a generalist group would drive past.

None of this requires a more expensive vehicle. It requires the vehicle to be yours.

Who a Private Safari Suits Best

Some groups benefit far more than others.

Families with children

A private vehicle lets the day bend around naps, snack stops and shorter drives. It also keeps the experience engaging for children, since the guide can pitch explanations at the right level and stop at the animals that actually hold their attention. For families, a private vehicle is close to essential rather than a luxury.

Photographers

Photographers want control over positioning, time and angle. A private vehicle can move to the other side of a sighting, wait for light, and stay long past the point where a shared group would want to move on. Beanbags and window seats matter more here than in any other style.

Multi-generational groups

Three generations in one vehicle have genuinely different needs. Older travellers may want shorter drives and more rest; younger ones may want long days. A private vehicle can split the difference, and can arrange a second vehicle for part of the trip when that is the better answer.

Anyone with a specific interest

Birders, botanists, photographers, walkers and wildlife specialists consistently report that a private vehicle is what makes a safari rewarding rather than merely pleasant. A generalist route is a poor fit for a specialist interest.

Couples who simply prefer quiet

Some travellers do not have a specialist interest at all. They simply want the vehicle to themselves, the freedom to sit in silence, and the option to skip an activity entirely. That is reason enough.

Choosing Your Driver-Guide

The guide is the single largest variable in safari quality, and a private vehicle puts you with one guide for the whole trip. That makes the assignment worth discussing at proposal stage rather than accepting whatever is available.

National licensing is the floor rather than the differentiator. What separates a good guide from an excellent one is time in a specific ecosystem, an ability to read behaviour rather than simply locate animals, and the judgement to know when to stay and when to move.

Ask how long the guide has worked in the parks on your route, whether they hold a specialist qualification such as birding or photography, and whether the same guide covers the whole itinerary. On longer trips spanning two countries, the guide usually changes at the border, which is normal and worth confirming in advance.

Vehicle Types and What to Ask For

Most private safaris use a closed 4x4 with a pop-up roof, typically a Land Cruiser or similar. A few regions use open safari vehicles with three rows of seats and no roof, which photographers generally prefer and which work well in private reserves.

  • Pop-up roof 4x4: The standard across East and Southern Africa. Good all-round visibility, protection from sun and rain, and roof access for photography.
  • Open safari vehicle: Common in private reserves such as Sabi Sand and the Mara conservancies. Better for photography and for feeling immersed, but exposed to weather.
  • Extended 4x4: Useful for families and larger groups, offering more legroom and easier movement between rows.
  • Fly-in light aircraft: Not a vehicle type in the driving sense, but relevant: internal flights allow 15 to 20kg in soft duffels, so hard cases stay behind.

Worth asking: Whether the vehicle is exclusive to your group for the full itinerary, whether a second vehicle is available if the group wants to split, and whether the guide stays with you across park boundaries.

Best Destinations for a Private Safari

Anywhere can be done privately, but some regions reward it more than others.

Northern Tanzania and the Kenyan conservancies reward a private vehicle because of the density of sightings and the length of the drives. A private guide can position for a Migration river crossing and wait, which is exactly the kind of day a shared vehicle cannot deliver.

Botswana's Okavango and Linyanti reward it because the days are long, the camps are remote and the rhythm is unhurried. Private concessions there also allow off-road driving, night drives and walking, which a private vehicle makes genuinely useful.

South Africa's private reserves around Kruger reward it because vehicles have freedom that national parks do not, and a guide who knows one reserve intimately will outperform a generalist on a large circuit.

Tanzania's safari regions and Kenya's safari regions both work well; the choice usually comes down to which Migration timing you want and whether you prefer a fly-in or a driving itinerary.

How Many Days to Allow

Four to five days is a workable minimum for one ecosystem. Seven to ten days is the range where a private safari becomes distinctly better value, because the guide has time to learn the group and the route can spread across two or three ecosystems without turning into a transfer marathon.

Two weeks allows a genuinely unhurried trip with a beach or city extension, though the law of diminishing returns applies after about twelve safari days. Most travellers report that day seven or eight is the high point.

What Drives the Cost

Four variables move the price more than anything else.

Group size: The single largest factor. A private vehicle costs roughly the same for two people as for five, so per-person cost falls sharply as the group grows.

Accommodation tier: The difference between a tented camp and a private-concession lodge is far larger than the cost of the private vehicle itself.

Season: Green-season rates typically run 20 to 35 percent below high season, with fewer vehicles at sightings. The wildlife logic is different but rarely worse.

Logistics: Flying between parks instead of driving saves days and costs real money. Long driving routes are cheaper and slower.

What does not move the price much is the guide. Good guiding is not where the budget gets squeezed on our trips.

Planning a Private Safari: The Process

Planning a private safari is straightforward, and the first conversation usually resolves most of the open questions.

  1. Share the basics: Dates, group size and composition, approximate budget and what you most want to see. Two or three sentences is enough to start.
  2. Receive a route proposal: A day-by-day outline with park sequence, accommodation suggestions and a line-item quote, usually within 24 hours.
  3. Revise freely: Most itineraries change two or three times. Pace, accommodation tier and park combination are the usual adjustments.
  4. Confirm and pay a deposit: A deposit of 20 to 30 percent secures camps and guides; the balance falls due 60 days before travel.
  5. Pre-departure briefing: Packing notes, health guidance, park rules and your guide's contact details arrive before you fly.

Because the trip is private, the plan can stay flexible right up to departure in a way that fixed group departures cannot.

Practical Notes and Packing

A private vehicle carries more space than a shared one, which changes packing slightly. Soft duffels are easier to stow, and a day bag that stays with you in the vehicle is more useful than a hard case.

Neutral colours are the standard recommendation and it is a real one, since tsetse flies in some regions are attracted to dark blue and black. A warm layer matters for early mornings even close to the equator, and a hat, sunscreen and insect repellent with 20 percent DEET cover the rest.

Photographers should bring a beanbag rather than a tripod, since tripods are awkward through a pop-up roof. A 100 to 400mm zoom covers the great majority of sightings, with a wide second body for landscapes.

Frequently Asked Questions About Private Safaris

What does a private safari actually include?

A private safari includes a 4x4 vehicle carrying only your group, a dedicated driver-guide who stays with you throughout, and a schedule you set with the guide each evening. It does not automatically include off-road driving or unlimited driving hours, since those depend on the concession and on park regulations.

Is every safari you sell private?

Yes. Every itinerary we operate runs with a private vehicle and a dedicated driver-guide. We do not sell mixed-group departures unless you specifically ask for a join-in price.

How much does a private safari cost compared with a group departure?

It costs more per person for a couple and can cost less per person for a family of five, because the vehicle price is fixed for the group rather than charged per seat. Run the numbers at your group size before assuming it is the expensive option. As a working figure, a private vehicle adds roughly 20 to 40 percent over a shared departure for two travellers, and often nothing at all for six.

Can we share a private vehicle with another family?

Only if you ask. Two families travelling together can share a vehicle and split the cost, which is a common and sensible arrangement. It stops being private the moment a stranger is placed in the vehicle, so we treat it as a decision you make rather than one we make for you.

Do we stop when we want to?

Yes. Departure times, drive lengths, rest breaks and meal times are agreed between you and the guide. Most groups settle into a rhythm of early starts and an afternoon break within the first day or two.

How many people fit in a private safari vehicle?

A standard 4x4 works comfortably for four adults, six with tighter spacing, and extended vehicles suit larger groups. Families with three generations sometimes run two vehicles for part of the trip so that the pace can differ. Tell us your group size and we will match the vehicle.

Can we request the same guide for the whole trip?

Usually yes within one country. Across a two-country itinerary the guide normally changes at the border, which is standard and worth confirming in advance. Where one guide can cover the whole route, we assign them.

Is a private vehicle worth it for a short safari?

On three days or fewer the advantage is smaller, because there is less time for the guide to learn your preferences and the route is usually fixed by geography. From four days upward it becomes one of the highest-value decisions you can make.

Are park fees cheaper on a private safari?

No. Park and concession fees are charged per vehicle per day or per person per day depending on the park, and they are the same whether the vehicle is private or shared. What changes is the vehicle hire and the guide, not the park tariffs.

Can a private safari be budget-friendly?

Yes. A private guide and vehicle work just as well with tented camps and simple lodges as with luxury properties. The accommodation tier changes the comfort and the concession rules; it does not change whether the vehicle is yours. See budget safaris for the value end.

What happens on a long transfer day?

A private vehicle makes transfer days more comfortable, since the guide can stop for scenery, markets and wildlife along the way rather than driving through. Where a transfer is genuinely long, we recommend flying instead, and the trade-off between time and cost is one we set out plainly in the quote.

Do we still get expert guiding in a private vehicle?

Yes, and usually more of it. A dedicated guide works with you continuously, which lets them build on what they learn about your interests each day. Our driver-guides average a decade in their parks, hold national guiding licences plus first-aid certification, and are assigned by specialty where it matters.

Can we change the plan mid-trip?

Often, yes. Because the vehicle and guide are yours, small changes can usually be made on the ground, subject to camp availability and park rules. Larger changes such as extending the trip need notice, but the flexibility is far greater than on a fixed departure.

How far ahead should we book a private safari?

Six to nine months for high season and Migration periods, when the best camps and guides are assigned first. Green-season trips can often be arranged with far less notice. Gorilla permits and balloon flights are the exceptions and should be discussed as early as possible.

Design Your Own Private Safari

A private safari is not a luxury label. It is a decision about how the days should run: your own vehicle, a guide who works to your interests, and the freedom to stay at a sighting for as long as it is worth staying.

Start with your dates, your group size and the two or three things you most want to see. From there the route, the accommodation and the pace follow, and the plan can keep changing until it is right.
